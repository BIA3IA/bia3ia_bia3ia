# Bianca's Very Normal Place

> *Nothing here is haunted. The cat has signed the appropriate paperwork.*

## Living Design Document

**Version:** 0.7  
**Status:** Early Game Bible / Living Document  
**Project type:** Interactive portfolio disguised as a whimsical narrative RPG  
**Primary language:** English  
**Public title:** *Bianca's Very Normal Place*  
**World name:** Intentionally unnamed  
**Common references:** *here*, *the village*, *this place*, *our normal place*

---

# 1. Project Vision

## 1.1 What this project is

*Bianca's Very Normal Place* is an interactive portfolio presented as a small explorable RPG world.

The visitor does not browse a conventional list of pages, skills and projects. Instead, they discover Bianca through a village built from her ideas, interests, projects, unfinished experiments, memories, aesthetic preferences and personal oddities.

The portfolio is not contained inside the world.

**The world itself is the portfolio.**

The visitor should feel as though they have stumbled upon a place that already existed before they arrived and will continue to exist after they leave.

## 1.2 Why this world exists

This world exists to express Bianca as completely and honestly as possible.

It is a place where she can:

- show who she is without reducing herself to a resume;
- give physical form to her ideas and projects;
- combine technology, magic, humor and mystery;
- create the sort of place in which she would genuinely love to live;
- make something deeply personal, imaginative and strange;
- demonstrate creativity through experience rather than explanation.

The project should feel unmistakably like Bianca.

It should not merely describe her personality. It should behave like it.

## 1.3 Central philosophy

> **The world was never waiting to be completed.**

The village is not damaged, defective or unfinished in a negative sense.

Its incompleteness is its natural state.

It does not desire a final version. It does not consider growth a temporary phase before completion. It enjoys changing, expanding and becoming something new.

The village exists.

It is happy to exist.

It particularly enjoys growing, receiving visitors and discovering new parts of itself.

Bianca changes, learns, abandons ideas, begins new projects and returns to old ones. The village changes with her.

Its beauty lies in becoming, not in being finished.

## 1.4 Core thematic statement

> **Everything tells a story. Nothing tells the whole story.**

No single NPC, building, object or dialogue should fully explain the world.

Every element reveals something, but every explanation leaves room for doubt, interpretation and discovery.

The player learns about Bianca and the village through fragments rather than exposition.

# 2. Creative Pillars

The following principles define the identity of the project and should guide every artistic, narrative and technical decision.

## 2.1 The world is always becoming

Nothing is treated as permanently final.

Buildings may gain rooms.

Objects may appear after Bianca learns something.

Projects may evolve.

Paths may shift.

An apparently complete location may later reveal that it has been waiting for another door.

## 2.2 Incompleteness is not failure

An unfinished object is still meaningful.

An abandoned project is still part of Bianca.

A place does not need to be completed to deserve to exist.

The game should never ridicule unfinished work or treat it solely as wasted effort.

## 2.3 The absurd is normal

Talking furniture, enchanted software, unreliable time, sentient signs and celestial emails are ordinary facts of life.

NPCs do not stop to explain them.

The stranger the world behaves, the more casually its inhabitants accept it.

## 2.4 Normality is suspicious

When something behaves in a conventionally normal way, the inhabitants may become concerned.

Examples:

```text
"Have you seen that tree?"

"Yes."

"It hasn't said a word all day."

"...concerning."
```

```text
"That house has stayed in the same place since yesterday."

"Should we call someone?"
```

A silent tree is more alarming than a talking one.

A building remaining still may imply that something is wrong.

## 2.5 Magic and technology are one discipline

Magic is not an alternative to technology.

Technology is not an alternative to magic.

They are two vocabularies describing the same system.

Spells are code.

Potions compile.

Git is a ritual.

Branches may become literal branches.

Computers run on enchantments rather than operating systems.

A cauldron may complain about being behind `main`.

No one considers this unusual.

## 2.6 Mystery must survive explanation

The world may offer clues, but it should rarely offer complete answers.

Every answer should create at least one new question.

The player should never receive a definitive encyclopedia explaining every law, character and historical event.

## 2.7 Exploration replaces instruction

The player should discover rather than be directed.

There is no rigid route through the portfolio.

Important information can be found through curiosity, optional interaction and repeated visits.

The player is not expected to see everything in one session.

## 2.8 The world grows with Bianca

When Bianca learns, creates or changes, the village may respond.

A new skill does not always require a new building.

It may instead create:

- an object;
- an animal;
- a room;
- a machine;
- a plant;
- a passage;
- a new behavior in an existing location;
- a strange inhabitant.

Example:

Learning Python may cause a small serpent to appear somewhere in the village.

## 2.9 The world does not need a destination

The village does not have a final form.

The visitor cannot complete it.

Bianca cannot complete it.

The project may eventually reach stable public versions, but the fiction should never suggest that the world has become permanently finished.

## 2.10 Tone inspiration without imitation

The project may draw inspiration from the tonal qualities of whimsical narrative games: unusual timing, surreal jokes, memorable character dynamics, playful fourth-wall awareness and emotional contrast.

It must not copy characters, dialogue structures, visual identities, plot devices or protected material from existing games.

Its voice must remain original and specifically connected to Bianca's world.

# 3. Emotional Direction

## 3.1 Five defining words

The current five-word definition is:

- Whimsical
- Weird
- Incomplete
- Magical
- Mysterious

The fifth word may later be refined into a more specific term expressing the desire to discover secrets.

Possible alternatives:

- Curious
- Secretive
- Enigmatic
- Discoverable
- Unknowable

**Final choice:** TODO

## 3.2 Intended emotional effect

After exploring the village, the visitor should feel:

- welcomed into a strange private world;
- amused by its absurd internal logic;
- nostalgic for a place they have never visited before;
- gently melancholic, but not sad;
- slightly uncomfortable in an intriguing and charming way;
- curious about what they missed;
- motivated to search for easter eggs;
- eager to return after future updates.

The desired reaction is something close to:

> "This is beautiful, but none of it is normal."

Followed by:

> "What was Bianca thinking when she made this?"

And then:

> "I need to see what else is hidden here."

## 3.3 What the tone must avoid

The project must never become:

- a corporate portfolio;
- a conventional business website wearing a game skin;
- a linear guided tour;
- a sequence of compulsory exposition scenes;
- a wall-of-text experience;
- relentlessly cheerful or sugary;
- emotionally flat;
- a horror game;
- a combat-focused RPG;
- a grind-based game;
- an imitation of another title;
- a museum where projects are displayed without interaction;
- a world that explains every mystery.

The village may be unsettling, but not threatening.

It may be melancholic, but not hopeless.

It may be playful, but not shallow.

# 4. Player and Possession

## 4.1 Who the visitor is

The identity of the visitor is deliberately undefined.

They are a curious outsider who has somehow found the village.

The game does not explain:

- where they came from;
- why they can enter;
- what form they normally possess;
- whether they are human;
- whether they were invited;
- whether this has happened before.

Upon entering, the visitor temporarily takes control of Bianca.

The exact mechanism is intentionally left unexplained.

## 4.2 Opening text

```text
Welcome to Bianca's Very Normal Place.

Bianca is currently unavailable,
so you may control her temporarily.

Please return her before midnight.

The cat has been informed.

He did not care.
```

## 4.3 Bianca's awareness

Bianca both knows and does not know that she is being controlled.

She may:

- act normally;
- ignore the visitor's presence;
- make comments suggesting full awareness;
- pretend nothing unusual is happening;
- briefly address the player;
- become more direct after a long period of exploration.

This contradiction should never receive a definitive explanation.

Possible late-session line:

```text
You've been here for quite a while.

I think you should go now.

Ryuk is starting to get agitated.

He only does that when something has stayed
where it doesn't belong for too long.
```

Alternative versions: TODO

## 4.4 The player cannot lose

There is no traditional failure state.

The player cannot permanently damage the village.

There is no combat defeat, game over or punishment for exploration.

Curiosity should be encouraged.

Actions may produce strange consequences, but never consequences that make the player regret interacting with the world.

# 5. Narrative Structure

## 5.1 No traditional narrator

There is no external narrator describing events.

The world narrates itself.

Storytelling comes from:

- NPCs;
- objects;
- signs;
- rooms;
- architecture;
- weather;
- visual changes;
- item descriptions;
- environmental jokes;
- repeated interactions;
- contradictions;
- silence;
- things appearing where they were not before.

No voice has total authority.

## 5.2 Fragmented truth

Different characters may interpret the same event differently.

One NPC may behave as though the visitor is Bianca.

Another may know that Bianca brings visitors.

Another may suspect possession but refuse to acknowledge it.

Another may openly speak to the player and then deny doing so.

Contradictions are part of the world's identity.

## 5.3 NPC knowledge

The inhabitants know more than they reveal.

Possible attitudes include:

### Treating the visitor as Bianca

```text
"Good evening, Bianca."

"Is time moving today,
or has it stopped again?"
```

### Quietly acknowledging recurring visitors

```text
"Bianca, honestly.

You keep bringing home the strangest people."
```

### Knowing the truth but avoiding it

```text
"You look different today."

"...No, not physically."
```

### Pretending not to know

```text
"Welcome back, Bianca."

...

"That is still the name we're using, yes?"
```

## 5.4 Fourth-wall awareness

The project may break the fourth wall frequently, but at different levels.

### Level 1: technical jokes inside the fiction

```text
"This room is still rendering."
```

```text
"The basement stopped working after the last update."
```

### Level 2: awareness of being software

Characters and objects may refer to:

- commits;
- builds;
- branches;
- loading;
- collision;
- save data;
- patches;
- version changes;
- browser behavior.

These concepts are treated as natural laws of the village.

### Level 3: direct awareness of the player

Rarely, an object or character may acknowledge that the visitor exists outside the world.

These moments should be brief, strange and unresolved.

Example:

```text
"You aren't from here."

...

"Neither is Bianca."
```

Level 3 moments must remain rare enough to feel significant.

# 6. The World

## 6.1 Current geographic scale

The world should feel like a small realm rather than a city.

Provisional scope:

- approximately 10-15 principal buildings;
- a central village;
- a town square;
- a forest;
- a lake;
- a cemetery/archive;
- an observatory;
- a coffee shop;
- a garage;
- Bianca's house;
- project-related buildings;
- hidden and shifting areas.

The total world should be compact enough to feel intimate and detailed.

## 6.2 Population

The village has approximately ten significant inhabitants at launch.

Not every character must be human.

Possible inhabitants include:

- humans;
- witches;
- spirits;
- ghosts;
- robots;
- cats;
- Death;
- talking objects;
- sentient plants;
- impossible beings with no clear category.

Minor ambient creatures and object personalities do not count toward the principal NPC total.

## 6.3 Living-world behavior

The village is active even when the player is not progressing toward a goal.

Possible systems:

- NPC routines;
- inhabitants returning home;
- weather transitions;
- Ryuk wandering, sleeping, watching or disappearing;
- lights switching on and off;
- objects changing dialogue;
- rare events;
- buildings subtly changing position after reopening the site;
- different details appearing on repeat visits.

The player should occasionally suspect that something happened while the browser was closed.

## 6.4 Changing geography

Buildings do not walk around on visible legs.

However, the arrangement of the village may change between sessions.

A house may be one tile farther left.

A path may curve differently.

A structure may exchange places with another.

Most inhabitants consider this normal.

A building remaining in precisely the same position for too long may be considered unusual.

# 7. The Identity of the Village

## 7.1 A place without a true name

The village has no canonical name.

This is not an omission waiting to be corrected. It is a deliberate part of the world's identity.

Names define things. They suggest that a place has become stable enough to be described, catalogued and distinguished from everything around it. This village does not wish to reach that condition.

It is normally called:

- here;
- the village;
- this place;
- our place;
- our normal place;
- *Bianca's Very Normal Place*, primarily when speaking to visitors.

Different inhabitants may offer different answers when asked for its name.

```text
"What is this place called?"

"Here."
```

```text
"Does the village have a name?"

"Not yet."
```

```text
"We almost chose one once."

"What happened?"

"We finished the discussion."
```

```text
"It is impolite to name something
that is still growing."
```

No hidden document should reveal a definitive true name.

The lack of a name is not itself a puzzle to solve.

## 7.2 Emergent consciousness

The village possesses a consciousness, but it does not appear as a single speaking character.

It has no central face, voice or body.

Its awareness emerges through the combined behavior of the world:

- houses react;
- doors remember;
- paths make choices;
- signs lie;
- rooms prepare themselves;
- objects notice the player;
- geography develops preferences;
- NPCs remember impossible pasts;
- weather arrives with suspicious timing;
- buildings alter their position between visits.

The village never needs to announce:

```text
"I am alive."
```

It should be understood as alive because it behaves as though it is.

Whether there is literally one mind behind every object or whether the collective behavior of many smaller consciousnesses merely produces the effect of a single mind must remain uncertain.

## 7.3 Bianca and the village appeared together

Bianca did not construct the village brick by brick.

The current guiding theory is that Bianca and the village appeared together.

At some point, without a known cause, both simply existed.

Bianca may have woken up there.

The village may have formed around her at the same moment.

It may be part of her consciousness, a world shaped by her consciousness, or a real place that happens to resemble everything inside her.

The project must never confirm which interpretation is correct.

There is no reliable account of:

- what existed before Bianca;
- what existed before the village;
- whether there was a before;
- how Bianca arrived;
- whether she arrived at all;
- whether the world was generated with her;
- whether Bianca was generated with the world.

## 7.4 Contradictory childhoods

The inhabitants behave as though they have always known Bianca.

Some remember her as a child.

Some remember only the present version of her.

Some claim she grew up in a house that did not exist during the player's previous visit.

Some remember teaching her things they could not possibly know.

Some remember the exact day she arrived, but disagree about the year, weather, age and direction from which she came.

A useful world rule is:

> **When the village creates something, it may also create enough past for that thing to appear as though it has always existed.**

This generated past is imperfect.

Contradictory memories are not necessarily errors. They are evidence that the world is still constructing itself in both directions.

## 7.5 What the village wants

The village does not want to be completed.

It does not seek a final form, perfect order or a definitive explanation.

Its primary desire is simply:

> **to exist.**

It enjoys:

- growing;
- changing;
- receiving visitors;
- expressing Bianca;
- discovering new versions of itself;
- retaining ideas that mattered;
- remaining open to what may appear next.

It cares deeply for Bianca because it grows with her and through her.

The affection between Bianca and the village should feel mutual without becoming sentimental exposition.

## 7.6 The world narrates itself

There is no external narrator.

The village's consciousness communicates through everything inside it.

A line on a sign, the absence of a tree's reply, a moved building, an NPC's false memory and a room's changed furniture are all forms of narration.

The world does not have to speak in one consistent voice.

Its consciousness is expressed through a chorus of objects, places and inhabitants.

This preserves the principle:

> **Everything tells a story. Nothing tells the whole story.**

## 7.7 The village can lie

The village is capable of deliberate deception.

Its lies are not limited to confused memories.

A sign may knowingly point in the wrong direction.

A house may insist that it has always stood in its current location.

An NPC may invent a childhood event because the truth is less entertaining.

Ryuk may provide an accurate answer in a way that makes it sound false.

Lies may serve several functions:

- humor;
- protection;
- resistance against Light;
- preservation of mystery;
- social play;
- avoidance of uncomfortable truths;
- keeping the world from becoming fully defined.

The player should be warned early that testimony is unreliable, but the warning itself may be unreliable.

```text
A small notice for visitors:

The village is generally truthful.

The village wrote this notice.
```

```text
Some residents may lie.

The residents requested that we clarify:
not them, the other residents.
```

## 7.8 Three sources of contradiction

When two statements conflict, at least three explanations are possible:

1. A character is deliberately lying.
2. A character remembers another version of reality.
3. The village changed the past and failed to update everyone consistently.

The game should rarely identify which explanation applies.

## 7.9 Visitors are expected, but never understood consistently

Bianca intentionally permits visitors to enter and temporarily control her.

She wants other people to see the village because she is proud of it and wants to share it.

The inhabitants do not perceive this process consistently.

Some see the visitor clearly.

```text
"Oh, another visitor."

"Bianca keeps bringing people here."

"She does like showing the place off."
```

Some perceive only Bianca.

```text
"Good evening, Bianca."

"Is time moving today,
or has it stopped again?"
```

Some see both at once.

```text
"You look like Bianca."

...

"From here, at least."
```

Some are uncertain whether every arrival is a new person or the same visitor returning through different versions of time.

No one knows how many visitors have entered.

## 7.10 Bianca lends her presence voluntarily

Bianca is not trapped or unwillingly possessed.

She knowingly leaves space for a visitor when she is unavailable.

The visitor temporarily receives movement and agency through her form.

The exact metaphysics are not defined.

Bianca does not normally interrupt a visit.

She may speak directly only when:

- she has returned;
- the visit has lasted long enough;
- Ryuk is becoming agitated;
- something has gone sufficiently wrong;
- the player has reached a highly specific secret.

Her rare direct voice should feel important.

## 7.11 Where Bianca goes

There is no canonical answer.

Inhabitants may suggest that Bianca is:

- constructing something new;
- sleeping;
- outside the village;
- in a parallel reality;
- visiting the player's world;
- watching through the cursor;
- hiding from Light;
- observing silently;
- inside a room that does not currently exist;
- still present, but temporarily distributed among everything the player is not looking at.

None of these theories should be confirmed.

## 7.12 Midnight is a deadline that never arrives

The opening asks the player to return Bianca before midnight.

Midnight is not a reliable hour.

It may not be an hour at all.

No one has observed it.

The clock tower cannot determine when it should occur.

The concept may have been inherited from a reality in which time behaved more conventionally.

Mezzanotte is therefore simultaneously:

- a warning;
- a joke;
- an impossible deadline;
- a ritual phrase;
- a boundary nobody can locate.

```text
"Has midnight happened yet?"

"No."

"Did it happen yesterday?"

"Also no."
```

```text
"Midnight?"

"Not yet."

"Not even earlier."
```

## 7.13 Leaving the village

There is no required in-world exit sequence.

The most truthful way to leave is simply to close the browser tab.

The village remains suspended rather than visibly ending.

NPCs may understand this.

```text
"How do I get home?"

"Close the tab."
```

```text
"The village tab."

"Preferably before you become like Bianca."

"She has one hundred and fifty open
and claims they are all important."
```

A menu option such as *Leave This Place* may respond:

```text
You already know how.
```

Ryuk may acknowledge departure in rare circumstances, but he should not perform a mandatory goodbye every time.

The act of closing the page should retain a slight feeling of abandoning a still-living place.

## 7.14 The village remembers returning visitors

The experience should preserve a local memory of each visitor when technically possible.

The village may remember:

- total visits;
- total time spent;
- objects examined;
- places entered;
- secrets discovered;
- choices made;
- dialogue branches selected;
- interactions with Ryuk;
- rare events witnessed;
- world changes triggered;
- objects or creatures caused to appear;
- whether the visitor tends to impose order or encourage strangeness.

Return dialogue may evolve.

```text
"You're back."

...

"You do like it here, don't you?"
```

```text
"Another visit?"

"At this point we should prepare a room."
```

## 7.15 Memory belongs to the local visitor

Persistent memory is tied to the browser or local device state.

A different browser, private session, cleared storage or new device may be treated as a different visitor.

This technical limitation becomes part of the fiction.

```text
"I knew you."

"I think."

"I may have left the memory
in another browser."
```

The game should not pretend to recognize a person when no local state exists.

## 7.16 Bianca remembers consequences, not necessarily events

Bianca may retain traces of what visitors did while controlling her.

She does not need to remember every interaction clearly.

She may instead remember:

- an emotional residue;
- a complaint from an object;
- a changed room;
- an inexplicable new responsibility;
- something Ryuk refuses to discuss.

Example:

```text
"I don't know who spoke to the cauldron yesterday,
but it is now demanding paid leave."
```

This lets Bianca respond to player actions without resolving how conscious she was during the visit.

## 7.17 Geographic anchors

Three locations remain stable enough to orient the player:

- Bianca's House;
- the Town Square;
- the Clock Tower.

These are the village's current geographic anchors.

Everything else may shift around them.

The anchors do not prove that absolute space exists. They merely behave more consistently than the rest of the map.

## 7.18 Shifting geography

Between sessions, non-anchor locations may:

- exchange positions;
- move several tiles;
- change apparent distance;
- receive a different path;
- temporarily disappear;
- reveal a new entrance;
- connect to a different neighbor;
- return with a slightly altered exterior.

Paths may change shape while still leading to known regions.

Some secrets may move completely.

Buildings must never visibly grow legs and walk around unless a future joke very specifically calls for it. Their movement occurs outside direct observation.

## 7.19 Change can be arbitrary or caused

Most minor changes do not require an explanation.

The village may move something because it felt like doing so.

Other changes may be linked to:

- interacting with a specific object;
- Ryuk crossing a particular road;
- a dialogue choice;
- rain;
- a rare moon event;
- returning after a project update;
- the visitor's tendency to organize or disturb;
- Bianca learning something new.

The coexistence of random and caused changes prevents the world from becoming mechanically predictable.

## 7.20 Geography may mislead, but not frustrate

The map is allowed to lie.

It is not allowed to make ordinary exploration irritating.

Known destinations must remain reachable.

Changed routes should create curiosity, not repeated dead ends.

The player should feel:

> "The village moved this."

Not:

> "The navigation is badly designed."

# 8. Light

## 8.1 Nature

Light is the central theoretical threat to the village.

It is not an NPC.

It has no body, face or physical form.

It is not simply sunlight.

It is a conceptual force born from the human desire to finish everything.

Every urge to make something final, resolved, perfectly ordered, fully explained and permanently complete contributes to the possibility of Light.

Light is usually referred to simply as:

> **Light**

It has no deeper true name.

## 8.2 Light believes it is helping

Light does not understand itself as malicious.

It believes the village is suffering from disorder, uncertainty and incompletion.

It wants to save the village by:

- stabilizing it;
- explaining it;
- organizing it;
- correcting contradictions;
- removing ambiguity;
- completing everything that remains open.

Its promise is sincere:

> "I will make you complete."

The horror lies in the fact that the village does not want to be saved in this way.

## 8.3 Completion as erasure

If Light succeeds, the village will not explode or visibly die.

It will become complete.

That outcome is equivalent to the loss of everything that makes it alive.

Light would:

- lock every building into one permanent position;
- force every path to remain identical;
- stop objects from speaking;
- make furniture inert;
- make time linear, measurable and correct;
- cause the Clock Tower to display a real hour;
- eliminate ghosts and spirits;
- remove magic from technology;
- prevent potions from compiling;
- turn rituals into ordinary commands;
- erase *The House of Almost*;
- require every project to be finished or discarded;
- give the village a definitive name;
- make deliberate lies impossible;
- reconcile every contradictory memory;
- end the permanent night;
- prevent Bianca from lending herself to visitors;
- exclude future visitors;
- explain every mystery;
- reveal every easter egg;
- remove hidden areas;
- define one authoritative version of every story;
- prevent the village from becoming anything else.

The consequence is not destruction through chaos.

It is destruction through finality.

## 8.4 The illuminated area

Light has reached one small, contained part of the village.

This area proves that the threat is real without allowing it to dominate the whole experience.

Inside it:

- objects do not speak;
- descriptions are literal;
- furniture remains still;
- architecture is perfectly aligned;
- paths are straight;
- nothing changes between visits;
- NPC dialogue, if present, repeats;
- there are no hidden interactions;
- no item has a second meaning;
- the music has little or no variation;
- colors are pale, bright and overexposed;
- the interface may feel uncomfortably conventional.

The area should be disturbing precisely because it appears clean, safe, rational and finished.

## 8.5 Design of the illuminated area

The area must remain small enough to feel like an incursion.

Possible visual traits:

- white or warm-white surfaces with no night-mode equivalent;
- sharp edges;
- symmetrical layouts;
- static shadows;
- no visible stars;
- windows emitting excessive light;
- washed-out colors;
- signs with perfectly unambiguous instructions;
- standardized objects;
- no decorative clutter;
- no movement that is not functional.

Example interaction:

```text
A chair.

It is used for sitting.
```

No second line appears.

That absence should feel more unsettling than any supernatural description elsewhere.

## 8.6 How Light communicates

Light does not speak through a body.

It may communicate through:

- emails apparently sent by the sun;
- windows that are too bright;
- interfaces without dark mode;
- white letters;
- blank paper that acquires text;
- system-like notifications;
- reflections with no darkness;
- overexposed photographs;
- messages interpreted through tarot cards;
- perfectly formatted documents nobody remembers creating.

Its communication should feel polite, helpful and invasive.

## 8.7 Emails from the sun

Possible drafts:

```text
SUBJECT: A Brighter Future

Good morning.

This greeting will become accurate soon.

No action is required.
```

```text
SUBJECT: Completion Status

Several unresolved elements remain.

We are pleased to inform you
that this condition is temporary.
```

```text
SUBJECT: Dark Mode

Your request has been reviewed.

It has been declined.
```

## 8.8 The tarot intermediary

One NPC who reads tarot may claim to communicate with Light.

This does not confirm that the communication is genuine.

Possible interpretations:

- Light uses the cards;
- the reader is lying;
- the cards are warning the reader;
- the village is impersonating Light;
- the reader wants attention;
- all interpretations are true in different versions.

The cards may repeatedly produce imagery associated with:

- sunrise;
- perfect symmetry;
- blank white fields;
- completed circles;
- closed doors;
- a final page;
- a name written in permanent ink.

The tarot reader's full identity, personality and allegiance remain TODO.

## 8.9 What keeps Light out

Light has not completed the village because the village changes too quickly.

Its defenses include:

- Bianca continually beginning new things;
- new ideas appearing before old ones settle;
- buildings moving;
- paths rearranging;
- contradictory memories;
- unresolved stories;
- NPC lies;
- hidden easter eggs;
- visitors exploring and interpreting the world;
- the Moon sustaining the night;
- Ryuk actively guarding Bianca and the village;
- abandoned ideas retaining value inside *The House of Almost*.

Light cannot finish what refuses to remain one thing long enough to be finalized.

## 8.10 Ryuk and Light

Ryuk knows what Light is.

He disdains it.

He considers it dangerous, but also deeply irritating.

Light represents the expectation that everything must behave according to one correct definition.

Ryuk rejects that expectation completely.

He does not want to behave like a normal cat.

He wants to behave like Ryuk.

Light may be one of the few things capable of making him stop pretending to be ordinary.

His resistance may include:

- sitting in illuminated thresholds;
- blocking white letters;
- deleting emails by walking across the keyboard;
- carrying unfinished objects back into the village;
- staring directly at overbright windows until they dim;
- relocating paths before Light can map them;
- refusing to explain any of this.

Ryuk should never deliver a full lore monologue about Light.

## 8.11 The Moon and Light

The Moon opposes Light and preserves the permanent night.

The Moon's exact power is unclear.

It may:

- shield the village;
- absorb incoming brightness;
- warn inhabitants;
- distort time;
- hide locations;
- communicate through reflections;
- blink when Light is near;
- cooperate with Ryuk;
- remember previous incursions.

The Moon is not necessarily a warrior.

Its resistance may consist simply of continuing to exist where sunrise is expected.

## 8.12 Bianca and Light

Light believes it can help Bianca.

It may view her as:

- incomplete;
- overextended;
- full of abandoned ideas;
- insufficiently organized;
- responsible for the village's instability;
- someone who deserves to be "fixed."

It wants to complete Bianca in the same way it wants to complete the village.

Bianca rejects this.

To complete her would be to remove her ability to change, begin, abandon, reconsider, experiment and become someone new.

The threat is therefore personal without requiring Light to hate her.

## 8.13 Lies as protection

The world's dishonesty is a defense mechanism.

Light requires:

- one truth;
- one stable history;
- one correct interpretation;
- one final answer.

Every contradiction makes the village harder to finalize.

Every lie creates another possible version.

NPCs may not consciously understand this, but their unreliability helps sustain the world.

Ryuk may understand it completely.

## 8.14 Visitors keep the world alive

Visitors help resist Light by:

- exploring;
- interpreting;
- discovering different paths;
- noticing different details;
- making different choices;
- producing multiple versions of the experience;
- returning with incomplete memories;
- keeping secrets active by continuing to search for them.

Every visitor creates another perspective.

A world with many interpretations is harder to reduce to one final version.

## 8.15 The player can influence the boundary

The player may be able to push Light back or help it spread slightly.

This should not create a conventional good/bad route or a permanent failure state.

Actions that may strengthen Light:

- aggressively sorting objects;
- choosing only literal explanations;
- demanding definitive answers;
- closing every open possibility;
- trying to make rooms perfectly symmetrical;
- responding that unfinished things have no value;
- repeatedly selecting certainty over curiosity.

Actions that may weaken Light:

- embracing ambiguity;
- protecting unfinished objects;
- choosing playful or open-ended responses;
- restoring disorder where it has been sterilized;
- following strange paths;
- interacting with overlooked details;
- helping objects regain their voices;
- accepting multiple conflicting stories.

If enough resistance actions occur, part of the illuminated area may darken again.

Objects may regain speech.

Color may return.

A hidden interaction may reappear.

If the player strengthens Light, the bright boundary may expand slightly, but never enough to destroy the entire village.

## 8.16 Restoration of an illuminated place

A recovered location should not return to its exact previous state.

It should come back changed.

This reinforces that resisting completion is not the same as restoring a perfect past.

Possible restoration sequence:

1. A formerly literal object produces an unnecessary second sentence.
2. One tile shifts out of alignment.
3. A shadow appears.
4. The music gains a wrong note.
5. A sign contradicts itself.
6. An object begins speaking again.
7. The area returns to night.

The first lie told by a restored object may be treated as a sign of recovery.

## 8.17 The one inhabitant who wants Light

Exactly one principal NPC sincerely wants Light to succeed.

This character is not evil.

They are tired.

They may be exhausted by:

- unreliable memories;
- visitors;
- permanent darkness;
- moving buildings;
- unanswered questions;
- inconsistent time;
- never knowing what is real.

They want:

- a correct clock;
- a stable home;
- a clear identity;
- one trustworthy answer;
- morning.

Their desire should be understandable.

They provide the strongest argument in favor of Light and prevent the conflict from becoming morally simplistic.

## 8.18 Light must not become a conventional villain

Light should not:

- laugh maliciously;
- deliver threats;
- command an army;
- chase the player through levels;
- become a final boss;
- possess a human-shaped form;
- explain its full origin;
- be permanently defeated.

Its menace comes from its sincere certainty that completion is always good.

## 8.19 The mystery cannot be solved

There is no final canonical explanation of Light, Ryuk, the visitors or the village's origin.

The player may discover patterns and strong theories.

They may push the illuminated boundary back.

They may learn why certain inhabitants are afraid.

They may hear Ryuk say something unusually direct.

But no ending should resolve the cosmology.

Uncertainty is not a missing final chapter.

Uncertainty is the final condition.

## 8.20 Central mystery network

The major mysteries are connected:

- What is Light?
- Why can Bianca be controlled?
- Where do visitors come from?
- What is Ryuk?
- What is the Moon?
- Did Bianca create the village, or did it create her?
- Why do contradictory pasts exist?
- Has midnight ever happened?
- Is there a finished version of the village somewhere?
- What happens if Light gives the village a name?

These questions may share clues without sharing one final answer.

## 8.21 Narrative presentation rules for Light

1. Light should be foreshadowed before being named.
2. Its presence should first appear as excessive normality.
3. The illuminated area should be optional.
4. Its messages should sound helpful.
5. No source should explain it completely.
6. The pro-Light NPC must be sympathetic.
7. Ryuk must react more seriously to Light than to ordinary absurdity.
8. Player resistance should restore strangeness, not simply darkness.
9. Light must never be permanently defeated.
10. The conflict must reinforce the value of incompleteness without declaring that finishing individual projects is bad.

## 8.22 Important thematic distinction

The game does not claim that completing a project is undesirable.

Bianca may finish projects, celebrate achievements and open completed buildings.

The danger is the demand that:

- everything must be finished;
- unfinished things have no worth;
- people must reach a final stable form;
- ambiguity must be removed;
- growth must eventually stop.

Light represents compulsory completion, not healthy accomplishment.

# 19. Geography and the Village Map

## 19.1 Geographic design objective

The village should feel medium-sized, dense and explorable without becoming tiring.

It must not resemble:

- a circular settlement built around one perfect center;
- a village stretched along one main road;
- a group of isolated houses scattered through a forest;
- a large open world filled with long empty walks.

It should instead appear to have grown organically and without a master plan.

Buildings sit near one another, but not according to a clean grid. Streets may bend unnecessarily. Small gaps, gardens, fences and paths create an irregular silhouette. The layout should feel accidental while remaining comfortable to navigate.

The player should be able to cross the ordinary surface of the village in a moderate amount of time. Density, interiors, secrets and route changes should create the impression of a larger place without requiring long travel distances.

## 19.2 Overall spatial structure

The village has a compact irregular core, surrounded by a small number of distinct edge regions.

The core contains:

- Bianca's House;
- the Town Square;
- the Clock Tower and its attached municipal facade;
- the Coffee Shop;
- Nyx's Garage;
- moving NPC homes and small shops;
- the future project-showcase structure;
- short paths and alleys that may rearrange between sessions.

The edge regions contain:

- the Forest and the Cemetery on one side;
- the Lake and Observatory on another side;
- The House of Almost near, but clearly distinct from, the Cemetery;
- the concealed road leading to the illuminated house;
- several different kinds of world boundary.

The village does not need to be geographically realistic. Emotional proximity matters more than conventional urban planning.

## 19.3 Perceived size and traversal time

The world should feel medium-sized rather than tiny or enormous.

Design targets:

- ordinary destinations should remain close enough that walking does not become repetitive;
- major areas should feel distinct without requiring long empty corridors;
- shortcuts should reward familiarity;
- repeat visitors should move through the world more efficiently than first-time visitors;
- interiors and secrets should supply depth that the exterior footprint does not need to provide.

Exact traversal targets must be tested after the first greybox map exists.

Provisional target:

- approximately 5-8 minutes to walk through the principal exterior regions without stopping;
- much longer to inspect objects, enter interiors and follow optional routes;
- no mandatory journey should feel like filler.

## 19.4 Geographic anchors

Three locations remain fixed between sessions.

### Bianca's House

Bianca's House is the visitor's origin point and one of the visual hearts of the village.

### Town Square

The Town Square is the primary routing space and social reference point, even though residents insist it is isolated and unimportant.

### Clock Tower

The Clock Tower remains visible from most outdoor regions and provides visual orientation without providing reliable information about time.

These anchors form the stable skeleton of the village.

Other locations may move around them.

The player should usually be able to recover their orientation by finding the tower silhouette, the square or Bianca's House.

## 19.5 First arrival and return position

On the first visit, the player appears directly outside Bianca's front door.

Bianca stands as though she has only just stepped outside, even though no departure animation or previous event is shown.

There is no welcome gate, tutorial attendant or introductory sign.

The world begins immediately.

On later visits, the player returns to the last remembered position from the previous session, provided browser-local state still exists.

If local state has been removed, the village treats the visitor as new and returns them to Bianca's front door.

This creates a quiet continuity between sessions: closing the tab does not reset the fiction. It merely suspends the visit.

## 19.6 World boundaries

The village uses four different boundary types. There is no single conventional perimeter.

### Boundary A: the dense forest

At one edge, the Forest becomes too thick to cross.

The Forest speaks in a welcoming voice and encourages the visitor to enter farther.

Other inhabitants advise caution because the deeper Forest may contain dangerously ordinary people gathering mushrooms.

Example warning:

```text
"Do not go too deep into the Forest."

"Why?"

"There may be normal people in there."

"Some of them collect mushrooms without asking the mushrooms first."
```

The threat is humorous, but the boundary should still feel uncertain and slightly dangerous.

### Boundary B: the threatening violet fog

At another edge, thick violet fog blocks visibility.

The fog is verbally hostile.

It does not politely explain the boundary. It threatens, mocks or warns the visitor when approached.

Possible lines:

```text
"One more step and I start describing what is inside me."
```

```text
"You cannot see through me."

"This is not an invitation to try harder."
```

The fog may remember repeated attempts and become increasingly irritated.

### Boundary C: returning paths

At another edge, paths appear to continue but eventually return the player to the village.

The reversal should not always be visually obvious.

A player may walk forward and arrive behind themselves, return to the Square or emerge near Bianca's House.

Signs along these paths may lie about their destinations.

### Boundary D: the undeveloped edge

At the final edge, the game openly acknowledges that the world does not yet exist beyond that point.

Canonical message:

```text
There is no invisible wall.

This part of the world has not been developed yet.
```

Alternative lines can appear on repeat attempts, but the message must preserve the idea that absence is development-in-progress rather than a conventional barrier.

## 19.7 Town Square

The Town Square is physically central but socially described as though it were an obscure and isolated location.

If the player asks why so few people are present, an inhabitant may respond:

```text
"Why would there be many people here?"

"This is a very isolated place."
```

This remains funny precisely because the Square is visibly the central junction of the village.

### Required square elements

- a central fountain;
- a strange cat statue inside or above the fountain;
- irregularly placed benches;
- lamps and warm pools of light;
- several direction signs, some accurate and some false;
- a small number of market stalls;
- the tarot reader's movable stall;
- at least one shortcut portal or marked teleport point;
- the Clock Tower and attached municipal facade along one edge.

### Population and atmosphere

The Square should not be crowded.

A few NPCs may pass through, sit on benches, inspect stalls or appear according to their routines.

The low population should make each encounter readable and memorable.

The Square should feel familiar, cozy and socially absurd rather than busy.

## 19.10 Bianca's House: exterior placement

- warm windows;
- signs of technology integrated with magic;
- a distinctive roof and silhouette;
- a small cat entrance;

## 19.11 Bianca's House: interior stability

Smaller objects may:

- appear after a new skill or project;
- vanish temporarily;
- move between rooms;

- react to visitor choices;
- claim that the player moved them previously.

New rooms may appear in future updates.

The interior should feel cluttered, personal, mixed and alive rather than carefully staged.

Interior dimensions do not need to match the exterior footprint.

## 19.12 The Forest

The Forest occupies one side of the map rather than surrounding the entire village.

The Cemetery lies near its edge, and the deepest visible trees become one of the world's hard boundaries.

### Emotional function

The Forest represents mystery, possibility and the danger of encountering something unexpectedly normal.

It should appear inviting before it appears threatening.

Trees, plants and fungi may speak casually.

The Forest itself may invite the player farther in, even though the initial playable world does not allow deep exploration.

### Tone

The Forest should not become horror.

It may feel uncertain and strange, with denser shadows and less reliable paths, but its danger is whimsical and conceptual.

### Launch scope

For the initial world, the Forest functions primarily as:

- a visible biome;
- the approach to the Cemetery;
- a source of dialogue and environmental jokes;
- a boundary;
- a location for future expansion.

A fully explorable deep-forest layer remains a future possibility.

## 19.13 The Lake

The Lake lies on the opposite side of the village from the Forest.

It is not an ordinary destination available through the most obvious road at all times.

A particular path, condition or world state may be required to reach it.

The Lake should feel discovered rather than merely visited.

### Visual direction

- water lilies;
- flowers near the shore;
- fireflies;
- soft magical lights;
- ritual-like arrangements of stones or plants;
- romantic and melancholic atmosphere;
- clear moon reflection;
- gentle environmental motion.

### Emotional function

The Lake represents reflection, possible outcomes and the emotional weight of choices.

It should feel peaceful, magical, romantic and slightly melancholy.

It is not primarily a recreational fishing location.

### Narrative behavior

The Moon's reflection may speak from the water.

The Lake may reveal:

- thoughts about past choices;
- possibilities that were not selected;
- different outcomes;
- memories that may or may not belong to Bianca;
- messages that only make sense after later discoveries.

It should not provide definitive answers.

Possible line:

```text
"The water shows you what might have happened."

"It declines to specify when."
```

## 19.14 Access conditions for the Lake

- following a temporary path;
- visiting after a particular dialogue;
- returning during rain;
- examining a specific reflection;
- following Ryuk without being instructed to do so;
- choosing a reflective rather than practical dialogue answer;
- using a shortcut symbol that appears only occasionally.

The final trigger should be discoverable but not mandatory.

The Lake must never become permanently inaccessible because of a wrong choice.

## 19.16 The Cemetery of Bad Ideas

The Cemetery is not the resting place of ordinary dead people.

Its emerging identity is the **Cemetery of Bad Ideas, Mistakes and Wrong Choices**.

It contains things that are not merely unfinished, but which are understood - fairly or unfairly - as poor ideas, failed judgments or paths that should not be resumed in their original form.

Possible contents:

- terrible project concepts;
- decisions Bianca regrets;
- features that created more problems than they solved;
- ideas whose only surviving value is the lesson they produced;
- choices that seemed excellent for approximately seven minutes;
- mistakes that have developed personalities.

The location should remain strange and bittersweet rather than cruel or self-punishing.

A mistake belongs here because it became part of Bianca's story, not because it deserves eternal shame.

### Discovery pattern

The player may arrive at the Cemetery accidentally.

Wrong turns, reversed paths and failed shortcuts may lead there more often than expected.

This is thematically appropriate: the Cemetery is a place reached through errors.

## 19.17 The distinction between the Cemetery and The House of Almost

The two places are geographically close and conceptually related, but their meanings must remain clearly separate.

### The House of Almost

Contains:

- good or meaningful ideas that were not completed;
- paused projects;
- promising experiments;
- things that might return;
- possibilities that still contain warmth.

Its central emotion is tenderness toward incompleteness.

### The Cemetery

Contains:

- mistakes;
- genuinely poor ideas;
- wrong decisions;
- paths that should not be repeated in the same form;
- failures preserved because they taught something.

Its central emotion is acceptance of error.

Canonical distinction:

> The House of Almost preserves what still might become.
>
> The Cemetery preserves what should not be forgotten merely because it went badly.

The proximity of these locations must not imply that unfinished work is dead.

## 19.25 The illuminated house

The first territory affected by Light is a single perfect house.

- clean;
- symmetrical;
- visually beautiful in a conventional sense;
- fixed in place;
- free of clutter;
- surrounded by orderly, attractive colored houses or facade elements;

The horror comes from perfection, not decay.

Inside or near it:

- objects do not speak;
- descriptions are literal;
- nothing moves unexpectedly;
- there are no hidden interactions;
- furniture remains exactly aligned;
- music is minimal or absent;

- repeated interactions produce the same response.

The illuminated house should initially be small in scope so that Light remains an approaching threat rather than a dominant biome.

## 19.28 Rules of movement

The village moves buildings because it wants to.

Most movement has no deeper mechanical cause.

This autonomy is essential: the village thinks for itself and is not a puzzle box controlled by the player.

Some specific changes may still be triggered by:

- an object interaction;
- Ryuk crossing a particular route;
- a dialogue choice;
- a real portfolio update;
- a rare weather state;
- an error-based secret.

These exceptions should not imply that the player controls the general geography.

The player cannot command a house to move or restore yesterday's arrangement.

## 19.29 Communicating geographic changes

The world should acknowledge movement through dialogue and environmental evidence.

Examples:

```text
"To reach the Coffee Shop, go straight and turn left."

"No, sorry. That was yesterday."
```

```text

```

```text

CURRENT LOCATION: HERE

PREVIOUS LOCATION: ALSO HERE

The handwriting suggests otherwise.
```

Possible visual evidence:

- empty foundations;
- compressed grass;
- paths ending at nothing;
- fences that no longer surround a building;
- abandoned door mats;
- signs corrected by hand;
- Ryuk sitting where a house used to be.

## 19.30 Paths

Paths are not individually conscious characters.

However, their behavior may still be unreliable because they belong to a conscious village.

Paths may:

- lead to the wrong destination;
- make opposite directions converge;
- loop back toward the Square;
- reflect yesterday's layout;
- shorten after repeated use;
- become a shortcut without announcing it;
- point correctly while nearby signs lie.

Path unreliability must never trap the player permanently.

## 19.31 No conventional map

The player does not receive a standard map.

In-world explanation:

```text
"A map?"

"Why would anyone draw one?"

"Everything would move before the ink dried."
```

The absence of a map avoids unnecessary maintenance and reinforces the village's identity.

Orientation instead relies on:

- visible anchors;
- compact distances;
- memorable silhouettes;
- NPC directions;
- repeated familiarity;
- shortcuts;
- automatic return routes.

The player may become lightly disoriented, but never permanently lost.

## 19.32 The village returns lost visitors

If a visitor wanders too long without reaching a meaningful destination, the village eventually returns them toward the central area.

Possible methods:

- a looping path opens onto the Square;
- a mirror appears;
- a shortcut symbol activates;
- Ryuk leads the player back without acknowledging it;
- the player exits one screen and enters near Bianca's House;
- an NPC gives directions that are surprisingly correct.

This system should be subtle rather than presented as an explicit rescue mechanic.

## 19.33 Shortcuts and teleportation

Fast travel exists through diegetic magical shortcuts rather than a conventional menu map.

### Marked ground points

Certain symbols, drawings or magical markings appear on the ground.

Stepping onto one can move the player to another known location.

The markings may:

- activate only after discovery;
- connect in non-obvious pairs;
- move between sessions;
- make small comments before teleporting the visitor;
- occasionally send the player somewhere more interesting than requested.

### Mirrors

Some mirrors permit destination-based travel.

The player may look into a mirror and express where they want to go.

The reflection may:

- arrive before the player;
- choose a slightly different destination;
- refuse a location associated with Light;
- remember where the player traveled previously;
- offer destinations the player has not yet visited.

### Shortcut design rule

Shortcuts should reward exploration and repeat visits.

They must not become mandatory before the player understands the surface layout.

## 19.34 Horizontal world, impossible interiors

The exterior village is primarily explored on a horizontal plane.

The initial game should not rely on:

- rooftop traversal;
- climbing the Clock Tower;
- large underground networks;
- deep basement exploration;
- platforming-focused vertical navigation.

However, interiors do not need to respect exterior dimensions.

A small building may contain:

- more rooms than physically possible;
- a corridor longer than the entire village;
- an internal garden;
- a room that appears outdoors;
- an impossible project floor;
- a door returning to a different building.

NPCs treat spatial impossibility as ordinary.

## 19.35 Error-based locations

Some secret places can be reached only through actions that resemble mistakes, glitches or incorrect navigation.

Possible triggers:

- entering the same room twice in an unusual sequence;
- opening a door at the wrong moment;
- walking a path backward;
- interacting with Ryuk many times;
- selecting a specific dialogue answer;
- closing the tab at a particular location and reopening it there;
- entering opposite paths that should not meet;
- using a mirror while the reflection is behaving incorrectly;
- approaching a boundary after it has already warned the player.

These should feel discoverable through curiosity, not arbitrary impossibility.

A player who never finds them must still receive a complete and enjoyable portfolio experience.

## 20.9 The Cartomancer

### Core identity

The Cartomancer is an exceptionally powerful witch and the village's principal tarot reader.

She is charming, elegant, mysterious and genuinely formidable.

Her weaknesses are not lack of skill or fraudulent knowledge.

Her weaknesses are distraction, poor explanation, lost trains of thought and occasional misinterpretation.

She may receive a perfect vision and then communicate it in the least linear manner possible.

### Bianca aspect

She reflects:

- Bianca's interest in tarot;
- intuition;
- powerful pattern recognition;
- distractibility;
- ADHD-like shifts in attention;
- the frustration of understanding something internally but struggling to explain it clearly.

### Appearance and silhouette

- adult witch appearing around twenty-five;
- large purple witch hat;
- intense violet and gold palette;
- layered mystical clothing;
- cards floating around her or appearing from sleeves, shadows and empty air;
- a silhouette readable from a distance through the hat and orbiting cards;
- subtle gold details that imply genuine power rather than costume theater.

### Location

She operates a mobile stall within the Town Square.

She may change position among the market stands, but she does not leave the Square during normal routines.

She never reveals where she lives.

Questions about her home produce contradictory or evasive answers.

```text
"Where do you live?"

"Mostly in the present."

"No, wait. That was not the question."
```

### Power

The cards are tools, not the source of her magic.

She can:

- summon and manipulate cards;
- perceive possible outcomes;
- open small portals;
- perceive traces stored in browser-local memory;
- recognize repeat visitors before ordinary NPCs do;
- sense Light earlier than other residents;
- withstand direct communication from Light;
- temporarily hold back or isolate a small illuminated region;
- notice when a dialogue, memory or location has been rewritten.

She should feel dramatically more powerful than her distracted manner initially suggests.

### Relationship with Light

Light can communicate directly only with The Cartomancer because she is powerful enough to survive and interpret the contact.

Light attempts to convince her that completion will save the village.

She does not accept this claim.

She listens because:

- ignoring Light would not stop it;
- she can monitor its intentions;
- the village needs someone able to hear the warning;
- no one else can safely sustain the contact.

Her readings are one of the principal routes through which the player learns that Light exists.

### Reading ethics

She tries to report what she genuinely sees.

She does not invent prophecies for drama.

She may frighten the visitor because she refuses to soften a dangerous reading.

However, she may:

- explain the cards in the wrong order;
- lose an important detail halfway through;
- confuse one possible future with another;
- become distracted by a card that should not be present;
- remember the warning but forget the reason;
- use a metaphor that only makes sense several visits later.

### Village tarot deck

Her deck is inspired by traditional tarot but belongs specifically to this world.

Possible cards include:

- The Unfinished House
- The Visitor
- The Cat
- The Closed Tab
- The Almost
- The Moving Door
- The Wrong Path
- The Clock Without Time
- The Moon That Blinked
- The Bug
- The Empty Grave
- The Final Version
- The Light

The final deck structure, suits and complete major arcana remain TODO.

### The hidden card

The Cartomancer owns one card she never willingly reveals.

Its image and meaning remain unknown.

It may not belong to the original deck.

Ryuk may know where she keeps it.

The card should support a long-term mystery rather than an immediate answer.

### Personal limitation

She cannot read her own future.

Any attempt results in:

- blank cards;
- contradictory spreads;
- cards belonging to someone else;
- Ryuk sitting on the reading;
- the same hidden card appearing face down.

This inability genuinely unsettles her, although she disguises it with confidence.

- accepts that a past decision cannot be changed;
- leaves one grave unopened for an entire session;
- recognizes that one bad choice caused a later good outcome;
- speaks about something happening in the present;
- admits that remembering is not the same as repairing.

### Interaction thread

1. Help rebury a decision.
2. Hear three incompatible versions of why it was bad.
3. Return after several sessions and find it uncovered again.
4. Discover a positive event connected to the mistake.
5. Choose whether to add that consequence to the headstone.
6. See him leave the grave alone once.

### Keepsake

A Certificate of Bad Decision, signed by Death and amended repeatedly by The Ghost.

### Voice examples

```text
"I once made a terrible decision."

"Which one?"

"I am still preparing the shortlist."
```

```text
"I know I cannot change it."

"I am checking whether it changed itself."
```

## 20.18 The Citizen

### Core identity

The Citizen is the village's only deliberately ordinary resident and its only supporter of Light.

He is a conventional office employee who lives in the perfect white house at the end of the hidden illuminated road.

He desires monotony, stable rules, fixed memories and architecture that remains where it was placed.

### Bianca aspect

He reflects the dangerous pressure to:

- become fully organized;
- remove contradiction;
- finish every task;
- know every answer;
- define oneself permanently;
- treat uncertainty as failure;
- trade imagination for stability.

He must remain understandable rather than cartoonishly evil.

### Appearance and silhouette

- completely ordinary adult human;
- white, beige and pale office-blue palette;
- perfectly pressed office suit;
- conventional hairstyle;
- correct posture;
- symmetrical sprite;
- no visible magic;
- no whimsical accessory;
- no deliberate visual oddity.

His normality is the unsettling feature.

### Home

He is the sole inhabitant of the perfect white house.

The house is:

- symmetrical;
- immaculate;
- brightly lit;
- fixed in place;
- free of speaking objects;
- free of secrets visible at first;
- organized around function;
- resistant to the village's shifting geography.

Other residents avoid the road and fear or distrust him.

### Discovery

The road and house do not appear immediately.

The Cartomancer reveals them after drawing **The Light** or another specific Light-related spread.

This event begins the visitor's deeper Light thread.

### Beliefs

The Citizen believes:

- truth must be demonstrable;
- lies are intolerable;
- magic is not real unless it can be measured;
- a house should stay in one place;
- memory should not change;
- time should move linearly;
- every process needs a final state;
- Light is rescue rather than destruction.

He does not understand that eliminating contradiction would also eliminate much of what makes the village alive.

### Relationship with truth

He claims he cannot lie.

He speaks literally and considers uncertain claims false.

However, his system produces its own distortion:

- absence of proof becomes proof of absence;
- metaphor becomes dishonesty;
- possibility becomes error;
- memory conflict becomes invalid data;
- magic becomes something he refuses to name.

He may be factually exact and fundamentally wrong.

### Fourth-wall awareness

He clearly recognizes some game and browser structures.

Unlike The Technomancer, he does not find them playful.

He interprets them as evidence that the village should be standardized, documented and completed.

### Relationship with Light

He is Light's only willing resident ally.

He believes Light will provide:

- reliable time;
- permanent identity;
- stable memory;
- predictable work;
- a fixed home;
- a final explanation.

He may not believe warnings that Light would remove magic, visitors, ghosts and living objects.

Alternatively, he may regard these losses as acceptable instability removal.

His precise level of awareness can evolve through dialogue.

### Relationship with Bianca

He believes Light can help Bianca become organized, stable and complete.

He frames this as care.

This makes him more disturbing than a resident who simply hates her.

### Relationship with other residents

Most residents avoid him.

He avoids the Coffee Shop because he dislikes unpredictable social behavior and gossip.

He conflicts with nearly everyone:

- The Cartomancer speaks in possibilities he cannot verify.
- The Apprentice changes subjects constantly.
- The Aspiring Mayor creates rules without enforceable systems.
- The Calculator calculates uncertainty instead of eliminating it.
- The Storm Witch causes weather through emotion.
- The Technomancer preserves bugs.
- The Barista tells gossip from other sessions.
- The Lakewatcher describes unrealized realities.
- The Ghost reopens settled decisions.
- Death admits uncertainty instead of pretending to possess final answers.

### Personal arc

He does not need to reject Light completely.

Meaningful changes should be tiny:

- tolerates one object being out of place;
- leaves a cup slightly off-center;
- admits that he does not know something;
- permits a small shadow to remain in the house;
- tells one minor lie to protect another person;
- recognizes that a stable memory can still be incomplete.

A small shadow in his house is a major narrative event.

### Player influence

The visitor may:

- reinforce his need for order;
- organize parts of the house;
- answer only in literal terms;
- help Light expand slightly;
- introduce ambiguity;
- leave an object out of place;
- tell a protective lie;
- restore one small speaking object;
- encourage him to tolerate uncertainty.

There is no final conversion and no victory state.

### Interaction thread

1. Unlock the road through The Cartomancer.
2. Enter the silent white house.
3. Speak with The Citizen.
4. Choose between order-enforcing and ambiguity-tolerating responses.
5. Notice small environmental changes across sessions.
6. See either a slightly brighter boundary or a returning shadow.
7. Witness him tell one protective lie in a rare branch.

### Keepsake

A perfectly white object with no description.

After certain interactions, the description may become:

```text
It is slightly less certain than before.
```

### Voice examples

```text
"The house has remained here for eleven consecutive visits."

"You are welcome."
```

```text
"Magic is not an explanation."

"Neither is uncertainty."
```

```text
"I did not lie."

"I temporarily withheld a fact from the Light."
```

## 20.19 Death

### Status

Death is a special principal figure outside the ten-resident count.

He is narratively accessible like an NPC but symbolically closer to Ryuk, the Moon and the village's major systems.

### Core identity

Death is a classic black-hooded figure carrying a scythe and an administrative clipboard.

He is solemn, deep-voiced, tired, ironic and unexpectedly gentle.

He handles the classification and burial of genuinely bad ideas, mistakes and wrong choices.

### Bianca aspect

He reflects:

- honest evaluation;
- acceptance;
- the need to stop reopening every mistake;
- the distinction between unfinished and harmful;
- protective realism;
- the ability to say "I do not know."

### Appearance and silhouette

- classic black hood and robe;
- face obscured or skeletal, final design TODO;
- large scythe;
- administrative clipboard or document folder;
- black and silver palette;
- solemn silhouette undermined by office supplies.

### Voice

His voice is deep and solemn but can become quietly sweet or protective.

He does not perform constant theatrical menace.

The seriousness makes bureaucratic lines funnier.

### Work locations

Death moves between:

- the Cemetery of Bad Ideas, Mistakes and Wrong Choices;
- The House of Almost.

He may keep:

- a cemetery filing station;
- a desk inside The House of Almost;
- transfer forms between the two locations;
- records proving why an item belongs in one place rather than the other.

### Duties

He:

- examines questionable ideas;
- issues Certificates of Bad Ideas;
- determines whether something is unfinished or genuinely bad;
- transfers misclassified objects from The House of Almost to the Cemetery;
- refuses to classify uncertainty as failure;
- attempts to prevent The Ghost from reopening graves;
- admits when a judgment is only provisional.

### Reliability

Death is one of the village's most reliable voices.

He says:

- "I do not know" when he does not know;
- "That is a hypothesis" when something is uncertain;
- "That is false" when he has evidence;
- "That may change" when the village has not finished deciding.

He does not know everything.

His honesty is valuable precisely because the world often lies.

### Certificates

Example document:

```text
CERTIFICATE OF BAD IDEA

Reason:
It seemed reasonable at three in the morning.

Status:
Properly buried.

Do not reopen without administrative cause.
```

### Secrets

Death possesses several private contradictions:

- He has a certificate related to the birth or first appearance of the village.
- He refuses to show it to anyone.
- He keeps one genuinely bad idea that he cannot bring himself to bury.
- He is fond of The Ghost and will not admit it.
- Light threatens to make his role meaningless by eliminating ambiguity, mistakes and incomplete classification.

The certificate must not provide a complete origin answer.

It may contain missing fields, contradictory dates or a signature that should not exist.

### Relationship with Bianca

Death is one of Bianca's closest residents.

He knows many of her bad decisions and protects them from becoming her entire identity.

He is capable of saying that a decision was bad without implying that Bianca is bad.

This distinction is central to his role.

### Relationship with Ryuk

Death treats Ryuk with professional respect.

He may refer to him as a colleague without explaining the shared profession.

Ryuk may sleep on official documents.

Death does not move him.

### Relationship with Light

Death opposes Light because:

- it would erase the category distinctions he protects;
- it would eliminate ghosts and conceptual afterlives;
- it would classify everything as complete or invalid;
- it would make honest uncertainty impossible;
- it would remove his purpose.

### Personal arc

Death may eventually reveal that he also struggles to let go.

His unburied bad idea and concealed certificate mirror the behavior he criticizes in The Ghost.

He does not need to resolve either mystery completely.

### Interaction thread

1. Receive a Certificate of Bad Idea.
2. Help transfer an object between The House of Almost and the Cemetery.
3. Rebury a decision opened by The Ghost.
4. Notice Death hiding an unfiled object.
5. Find evidence of the village-origin certificate.
6. Hear Death admit that he does not know what the certificate proves.

### Keepsake

A stamped administrative receipt confirming that one mistake has been processed and may now be left alone.

### Voice examples

```text
"I do not know."

"This is not a failure of authority."

"It is the correct answer."
```

```text
"A bad decision is not a bad person."

"Please stop digging it up."
```

## 20.20 Social relationships

### Bianca's closest residents

The three residents closest to Bianca are:

1. **The Sleepless Barista** - familiar daily comfort, coffee and conversation.
2. **Death** - knowledge of mistakes, honest judgment and protective acceptance.
3. **The Cartomancer** - readings, possible futures and deep magical trust.

This does not make other relationships unimportant.

It establishes the characters most likely to speak about Bianca with personal familiarity.

### Primary comic dynamics

#### The Cartomancer and Ryuk

Ryuk rearranges, hides and sits on her cards.

She accuses him of disrupting readings.

He may be correcting them.

#### Death and The Ghost

The Ghost digs up decisions.

Death reburies and refiles them.

Their argument repeats but slowly reveals affection.

#### The Citizen and everyone

The Citizen's literalism, order and support for Light conflict with almost every resident's mode of existence.

#### The Storm Witch and The Calculator

Impulsive action and endless analysis irritate each other.

Their rare successful collaboration demonstrates that balance can exist without either becoming someone else.

#### The Aspiring Mayor and the village

She attempts to govern a place that does not recognize fixed authority.

The village responds to her administration by behaving even less administratively.

#### The Apprentice and The Sleepless Barista

The Apprentice orders drinks she forgets to consume.

The Barista remembers each one and may resume conversations The Apprentice forgot beginning.

#### The Technomancer and The Calculator

One preserves bugs and unpredictability.

The other attempts to model them.

Both depend on the other's expertise.

## 20.24 NPC movement between sessions

Characters may appear in different appropriate locations between sessions.

Movement should respect identity:

- The Lakewatcher remains at or through the Lake.
- The Cartomancer remains within the Square.
- The Citizen remains in or near the white house.
- Death and The Ghost remain within their cemetery/Almost network.
- The Aspiring Mayor remains near municipal and public spaces.
- The Calculator and Technomancer remain connected to the Garage.
- The Barista remains primarily at *Just One More*.

Other residents can move more freely.

## 20.25 Visitor relationships

NPCs may remember the local visitor through browser-local state.

They can track:

- conversation count;
- selected responses;
- secrets known;
- returned keepsakes;
- whether the visitor helped or resisted Light;
- whether a personal interaction thread advanced;
- whether the visitor has been absent across updates;
- how often Ryuk was disturbed;
- whether the visitor listened or repeatedly interrupted.

The system should create additional dialogue and recognition, not conventional romance meters or numerical friendship bars.

## 20.26 Response choices

Dialogue choices should remain short.

Possible choice types:

- sincere response;
- strange response;
- silence;
- question;
- statement clearly belonging to Bianca;
- response that changes after being selected;
- option remembered much later;
- technically correct but emotionally inappropriate answer;
- deliberate lie;
- refusal to define something.

Characters may react differently to silence.

Silence is a valid dialogue action, not absence of interaction.

## 20.27 Small personal interaction threads

The game supports quest-like threads without conventional quest UI, arrows, checklists or reward popups.

Examples:

- recover a missing world-tarot card for The Cartomancer;
- choose a study topic for The Apprentice;
- attend The Aspiring Mayor's empty-square speech;
- help The Calculator make a trivial decision;
- recover a burned recipe page for The Storm Witch;
- catch or return a bug creature for The Technomancer;
- keep The Sleepless Barista awake long enough to finish a story;
- find the conditional path to The Lake;
- rebury one decision for The Ghost;
- leave one object out of place in The Citizen's house;
- deliver or receive an absurd certificate from Death.

These threads should:

- emerge through conversation;
- remain optional;
- tolerate interruption across sessions;
- avoid failure states;
- change dialogue or environment;
- sometimes remain incomplete by design.

- The Apprentice admits that one interest may remain paused forever.
- The Technomancer removes a dangerous bug while saving the harmless one.
- The Barista remembers a conversation from the correct session.

These events should not all occur in one playthrough.

## 20.31 Writing rules for NPC dialogue

1. Characters must have distinct rhythm and priorities.
2. Dialogue must not become a wall of text.
3. Emotional depth should emerge through small lines, pauses and repeated visits.
4. Technical jokes must remain understandable through context.
5. Characters must not explain which part of Bianca they represent.
6. Meta awareness must differ by character.
7. The Citizen's normality must remain more unsettling than overt villain dialogue.
8. The Cartomancer must be powerful, not incompetent.
9. The Apprentice's unfinished learning must be treated with affection, not contempt.
10. The Storm Witch's anger must remain brief, comic and non-abusive.
11. The Calculator's anxiety must not reduce him to a machine stereotype.
12. The Ghost may regret choices without making the game emotionally hopeless.
13. Death may be honest without becoming an exposition machine.
14. The Lakewatcher should be poetic but comprehensible.
15. The Barista's gossip may reveal lore without becoming a mandatory information desk.
16. The Aspiring Mayor must possess genuine courage beneath the joke.
17. The Technomancer's bugs should be lovable enough that fixing them is not always desirable.
18. No resident should fully solve the mystery of the village.

# 29. Dialogue, Interaction and Relationship Memory

## 29.1 Purpose of the dialogue system

Dialogue is one of the primary forms of gameplay in *Bianca's Very Normal Place*.

The game has no combat loop and no mandatory linear route. The visitor builds an understanding of the village through:

- choosing replies;
- remaining silent;
- leaving conversations unfinished;
- returning across multiple sessions;
- hearing characters contradict themselves;
- noticing changes in phrasing;
- listening to residents speak without Bianca;
- examining objects repeatedly;
- remembering information that the village may later deny;
- forming different relationships with different inhabitants.

Dialogue must never feel like a conventional portfolio page divided into speech boxes.

Every conversation should do at least one of the following:

- reveal character;
- create humor;
- expose a world rule;
- contradict an apparent truth;
- react to a remembered choice;
- deepen a relationship;
- introduce a secret;
- make the village feel active without the player;
- communicate portfolio information through personality rather than exposition.

The system should prioritize agency and reactivity without turning the experience into a branching visual novel with a fixed ending.

# 30. The Visitor's Voice

## 30.1 Choice is the default for principal NPCs

When speaking with a principal NPC, the visitor should frequently choose a response.

Automatic dialogue remains appropriate for:

- brief greetings;
- environmental comments;
- object descriptions;
- scenes in which an NPC is telling a story;
- short reactions that do not require input;
- conversations overheard between residents;
- moments in which Bianca refuses to let the visitor answer.

However, most meaningful principal-character conversations should eventually present a choice.

The preferred default is two to four concise options rather than a long list.

## 30.2 Standard response categories

A conversation may offer any combination of:

- a short direct reply;
- a sincere reply;
- an absurd reply;
- a knowingly false reply;
- silence;
- `Turn and walk away.`

Example:

```text
THE APPRENTICE:
Do browsers dream when all their tabs are closed?

> Probably.
> No.
> Mine never gets the opportunity.
> [Remain silent.]
> [Turn and walk away.]
```

Not every conversation requires every category.

## 30.3 The answer belongs to both visitor and Bianca

The visitor selects the option, but Bianca's body and voice deliver it.

The response is therefore a mixture of:

- the visitor's intention;
- Bianca's existing personality;
- Bianca's willingness to say the selected words;
- the NPC's perception of whether they are speaking to Bianca or to a visitor.

This ambiguity should remain unresolved.

## 30.4 Bianca may interfere

Bianca may rarely:

- refuse to speak a selected answer;
- alter one word;
- replace an answer with another;
- add a comment after the visitor's response;
- remove an option before it is selected;
- instruct the NPC to ask her later;
- answer without the visitor's permission.

Example:

```text
> I hate this place.

BIANCA:
No.

> This place is deeply concerning.
```

Bianca's interventions should feel personal and surprising. They must not occur so often that the visitor feels their choices are meaningless.

## 30.5 No free-text input

The visitor does not type unrestricted dialogue.

All answers are authored options because:

- choices must have designed consequences;
- the characters must remember specific statements;
- the dialogue voice must remain coherent;
- uncontrolled input would weaken timing and humor;
- the game is not an AI chatbot.

Rare scenes may visually imitate a text field, but it should still resolve into authored options or refuse to open.

# 31. Silence, Delay and Changing Options

## 31.1 Silence is a real choice

Silence is not equivalent to failing to interact.

It produces character-specific reactions.

Examples:

- The Cartomancer may interpret silence as consent to continue.
- The Apprentice may decide the visitor is taking too long and answer her own question.
- The Calculator may recalculate the probability that the conversation is successful.
- The Lakewatcher may interpret silence as a thoughtful response.
- The Citizen may reject silence as unverifiable.
- Death may accept silence without filling it.
- The Sleepless Barista may fall asleep inside it.

## 31.2 Timed silence

Some characters may react if the visitor waits before choosing.

The Apprentice may continue speaking after a short pause because she has already thought of another question.

This timing must respect accessibility settings. A player using slower text or input timing must not lose content because of an inaccessible countdown.

The fictional effect may remain, while the actual delay can be lengthened or disabled through accessibility options.

## 31.3 Options may change

Dialogue options may:

- change wording;
- exchange positions;
- disappear;
- appear late;
- contradict themselves;
- become more honest while being observed;
- become less honest while being observed;
- be covered or displaced by Ryuk;
- be replaced by Bianca.

This mechanic should appear often enough to become part of the world's identity, but not in every conversation.

## 31.4 Character-specific option behavior

### Frequent dynamic options

- The Cartomancer: options may change when a prediction shifts or she loses the thread.
- The Apprentice: questions may change before the visitor answers.
- The Calculator: wording may update as probabilities change.
- The Citizen: options may become increasingly literal.
- Ryuk-related scenes: Ryuk may cover, remove or select an option.

### Occasional dynamic options

- The Aspiring Mayor: titles and political labels may rewrite themselves.
- The Storm Witch: an answer may be interrupted by thunder.
- The Technomancer: a bug may alter a line.
- The Sleepless Barista: an option may become confused with another session.
- The Lakewatcher: an option may reflect a choice that was never made.
- The Ghost: an answer may become a regret before it is selected.

### Usually stable options

- Death: concise, reliable choices should generally remain readable and stable.

Dynamic options should support characterization rather than exist as random visual noise.

# 32. Beginning, Continuing and Leaving Conversations

## 32.1 Default initiation

Most interactions begin when the player approaches and presses the interaction control.

This keeps exploration voluntary and prevents the village from constantly interrupting movement.

## 32.2 NPC-initiated conversations

Only selected beings should frequently initiate contact:

- The Apprentice, when a new question occurs to her;
- Ryuk, through actions rather than conventional speech;
- the Fountain;
- certain talking objects;
- rare environmental events;
- an NPC involved in an active personal thread;
- Bianca during a late-session return event.

Other residents may occasionally call out a short ambient line without opening a full dialogue box.

## 32.3 Movement during dialogue

The standard rule is that both Bianca and the NPC pause during a full conversation.

Exceptions include characters occupied by a continuous activity:

- The Sleepless Barista may continue preparing coffee or sleeping.
- The Technomancer may continue adjusting an invention.
- The Storm Witch may continue stirring or arguing with the cauldron.
- The Aspiring Mayor may continue arranging papers during a speech.

Ambient conversations do not necessarily stop movement.

## 32.4 Walking away

The visitor may normally leave a conversation through:

- an explicit `Turn and walk away.` option;
- the cancel control;
- physically leaving during an ambient exchange.

The exact keyboard or controller button remains a technical decision.

The action should be explained clearly during the first relevant conversation.

## 32.5 NPC reactions to interruption

Characters react according to personality.

Examples:

```text
THE ASPIRING MAYOR:
I had seventeen further points.

They were the important ones.
```

```text
THE SLEEPLESS BARISTA:
That is fine.

I may already have been asleep.
```

```text
THE LAKEWATCHER:
You may leave.

The reflection will finish listening.
```

```text
THE CITIZEN:
This conversation has not reached
an approved conclusion.
```

## 32.6 Important conversations

A crucial scene may still be interrupted, but the game should not permanently lock required content.

Possible behavior:

1. The NPC comments that the visitor left during something important.
2. The conversation becomes available again later.
3. The restart may use altered wording.
4. The interruption remains remembered.
5. The player may lose a specific emotional variant, but not access to an essential mechanic.

The game should never force the visitor to remain in a very long unskippable monologue.

## 32.7 Remembering interruptions

Only some NPCs consistently remember being interrupted.

### Likely to remember

- The Cartomancer;
- The Aspiring Mayor;
- The Citizen;
- The Lakewatcher;
- The Ghost;
- Death;
- The Calculator.

### Likely to forget or ignore

- The Apprentice may forget because she moved to another question.
- The Sleepless Barista may be unsure whether the visitor left or he fell asleep.
- The Storm Witch may become angry and then forget.
- The Technomancer may blame a connection problem.

An NPC aware of visitors may ask whether the current player is the same rude visitor.

An NPC who sees only Bianca may frame the same memory as Bianca behaving strangely.

# 33. Repeated Interaction

## 33.1 Standard interaction ladder

Principal NPCs should generally support:

1. primary conversation;
2. secondary response or topic;
3. short repeat line;
4. reaction to insistence;
5. increasingly absurd repeat material;
6. rare secret or easter egg after exceptional persistence.

Important objects should also change responses after repeated examination.

## 33.2 Exhausted dialogue pools

When a character has no ordinary material left during the current state, they may:

- repeat a short phrase;
- ask to be left alone;
- invent a ridiculous new fact;
- reveal a tiny secret;
- pretend not to be an NPC;
- refuse to respond;
- respond through an object nearby;
- comment that the dialogue pool has become concerningly shallow.

The system should avoid making every NPC repeat the exact same line immediately.

## 33.3 Contradicting earlier dialogue

NPCs may deny or contradict past conversations because:

- they lied earlier;
- they are lying now;
- their memory changed;
- the village rewrote part of the past;
- the current speaker is a slightly different version;
- they do not want the visitor to know the truth;
- they genuinely misunderstood the first event.

Contradiction may occur regularly, but major emotional commitments should not be erased casually.

## 33.4 One-time dialogue

Some lines are intentionally available only once per local visitor identity.

These may include:

- rare acknowledgements of the visitor;
- a major character's first moment of trust;
- a unique Light encounter;
- a Moon event;
- an unusually direct line from Bianca;
- an answer from Ryuk;
- a sentence connected to the hidden card;
- a secret that changes how later lines are read.

There is no complete dialogue archive.

The visitor is expected to pay attention.

A full local reset may make a one-time line available again, but at the cost of resetting the visitor's entire remembered history.

# 34. Browser-Local Memory

## 34.1 Canonical persistence scope

The village remembers a visitor locally within the browser and site data used for that visit.

A different browser, profile or device normally creates a new visitor identity.

No account system is required for the initial version.

## 34.2 Required persistent information

The browser should remember at least:

- total visit count;
- first-visit and latest-visit timestamps where technically appropriate;
- last valid world position;
- previously generated world arrangement identifiers;
- NPCs encountered;
- interaction count per NPC;
- relationship values per NPC;
- significant answers selected;
- lies told by the visitor;
- conversations abandoned;
- objects examined;
- repeated-object interaction counts;
- secrets discovered;
- keepsakes received;
- Ryuk interaction history;
- Light influence and restoration state;
- rare events already observed;
- Moon events observed;
- dynamic buildings already introduced;
- personal-thread stages;
- one-time lines already consumed;
- optional accessibility and interface preferences.

## 34.3 Recommended persistence layers

A technical implementation may use:

- IndexedDB for structured state;
- localStorage for a small recovery marker and settings;
- sessionStorage only for temporary current-session data;
- a versioned migration system for future updates.

The canonical fiction should not expose these exact technologies unless used in a deliberate Technomancer joke.

## 34.4 State versioning

Every saved state should include a schema version.

Future releases must migrate older visitor histories where practical rather than silently erasing them.

If a migration cannot preserve a detail, the village may acknowledge the loss through fiction.

Example:

```text
A memory was moved during the update.

Nobody signed the permit.
```

## 34.5 Position recovery

On the first visit, Bianca appears outside her front door.

On later visits, she returns to the last valid position before the tab closed.

If that position no longer exists because geography changed, the game should place her at:

1. the nearest safe point in the same region;
2. the closest associated anchor;
3. Bianca's front door as the final fallback.

The village may comment on the correction.

## 34.6 Complete deletion of browser data

If the player completely deletes all site storage, a client-only game cannot reliably know that the same person visited before.

Redundant localStorage, IndexedDB, cache or service-worker records may detect a partial reset, but none can survive a true deletion reliably.

Cross-browser recognition would require an account, server-side identifier or invasive fingerprinting. These are not part of the intended experience.

Therefore the canonical rule is:

- a complete deletion creates a genuinely new local visitor;
- a partial or inconsistent reset may trigger an eerie residual-memory line;
- the game must not falsely promise supernatural recognition after all data is gone.

Possible partial-reset line:

```text
Something here remembers you.

Nothing can prove it.
```

This limitation should be treated as part of the world's logic rather than hidden through unethical tracking.

# 35. Hidden Relationship Model

## 35.1 Hybrid system

Relationships use a combination of:

- hidden numerical values;
- explicit event flags;
- personal-thread stages;
- remembered answers;
- state-specific conditions.

The visitor never sees hearts, meters, ranks or numerical affinity values.

## 35.2 Recommended hidden dimensions

Each principal NPC may track:

| Dimension | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Familiarity | How accustomed the NPC is to this local visitor or this version of Bianca |
| Trust | Willingness to reveal personal or important material |
| Curiosity | Interest in learning more about the visitor |
| Irritation | Frustration caused by interruption, cruelty, repetition or imposed order |
| Awareness | Degree to which the NPC recognizes a visitor rather than only Bianca |

Not every dimension changes for every character.

## 35.3 Numerical range

A practical implementation may use a hidden range such as `-100` to `100` or `0` to `100`.

The exact numbers are technical and should not be exposed in the fiction.

Dialogue should use broad state bands rather than checking dozens of tiny thresholds.

Example internal bands:

- unfamiliar;
- recognized;
- familiar;
- trusted;
- unusually close;
- knows too much.

## 35.4 Event flags remain essential

A high trust value does not replace specific history.

Examples:

```text
left_during_hidden_card_reading = true
lied_about_open_tabs = true
returned_ghost_decision = false
citizen_tolerated_misaligned_cup = true
ryuk_removed_answer = true
```

A combination of values and flags prevents relationships from becoming generic affection scores.

## 35.5 Affection and recognition

NPCs aware of visitors may distinguish:

- a new visitor;
- a returning visitor;
- a regular visitor;
- a trusted visitor;
- a visitor who has seen too much;
- a visitor associated with Light;
- a visitor Ryuk appears to tolerate.

NPCs unaware of visitors interpret the same progression as changes in Bianca's behavior or their own relationship with her.

## 35.6 Persistent irritation

Irritation may persist across sessions.

A character can remember:

- being repeatedly interrupted;
- cruel or dismissive answers;
- invasive questions;
- support for Light;
- mistreatment of another resident;
- a lie later proven false.

Irritation is not a punishment or permanent lockout.

It may decrease through:

- sincere answers;
- listening without interrupting;
- completing a personal interaction;
- returning a relevant object;
- protecting another resident;
- allowing time to pass across visits;
- accepting responsibility for a previous choice.

## 35.7 No universal morality meter

The game should not classify the visitor as good or evil.

Different residents value different behavior.

An answer that increases familiarity with The Ghost may irritate The Citizen.

A chaotic action may delight The Technomancer and concern The Calculator.

The visitor cannot maximize every relationship in one continuous history.

# 36. Remembering, Forgetting and Lying

## 36.1 Intentional forgetting

The village may claim to forget an event even when the data remains stored.

Possible causes:

- the NPC chose not to remember;
- the village is protecting itself;
- the NPC is lying;
- the memory is temporarily inaccessible;
- Light removed ambiguity from the memory;
- a later scene needs the memory to return.

## 36.2 Memory is not proof

A stored flag indicates that an event occurred in the visitor's experienced history.

It does not guarantee that every resident agrees it occurred.

The game may use the same remembered event to generate incompatible interpretations.

## 36.3 No lie indicator

The interface never marks a statement as true or false.

There is no:

- lie icon;
- special text color;
- truth meter;
- narrator correction;
- automatic journal entry exposing contradictions.

The visitor must compare dialogue, locations, objects and memories.

## 36.4 Reliability hierarchy

### Death

Usually reliable when distinguishing:

- known fact;
- hypothesis;
- rumor;
- missing information.

Death does not know everything and admits uncertainty.

### The Cartomancer

Sincere and highly capable, but fallible in interpretation, memory and explanation.

### The Citizen

Believes he states only truth, but rejects anything that cannot be directly demonstrated and is ideologically shaped by Light.

### Ryuk

Possesses the most accurate knowledge but does not owe anyone an answer.

### The village

May lie deliberately for humor, protection or preference.

### Objects

Each category has its own relationship with truth.

- Signs may intentionally misdirect.
- Doors may not know where they lead.
- Mirrors may tell the truth only to reflections.
- The Storm Witch's cauldron exaggerates during arguments.
- The Fountain may confuse public information with gossip.
- Keepsakes may remember their original owner differently.

## 36.5 The visitor may lie

Some authored responses are knowingly false.

The village may remember and repeat those claims later.

Example:

```text
THE APPRENTICE:
How many tabs do you have open?

> Three.
> Seventeen.
> I refuse to count.
> One hundred and fifty thousand.
```

Later:

```text
THE APPRENTICE:
You said three.

I wrote it down in six notebooks.
```

A lie may be funny, protective, cruel, strategic or accidental. It does not automatically reduce a universal morality score.

# 37. NPC-to-NPC Conversations

## 37.1 Independent social life

Residents speak to each other without Bianca.

The visitor may encounter:

- a conversation already in progress;
- an argument;
- gossip;
- a shared activity;
- two residents silently avoiding a topic;
- an NPC commenting on someone absent;
- a resident telling a different version of an event Bianca already heard.

## 37.2 Joining a conversation

The visitor may usually approach and interact.

Possible results:

- both residents address Bianca;
- only the interrupted speaker responds;
- the second resident corrects the first;
- the conversation ends abruptly;
- the visitor receives a joint choice set;
- one resident leaves;
- both pretend they were not discussing Bianca.

## 37.3 Conversation combinations

Most social encounters should be selected semi-randomly from valid combinations.

Some combinations require specific conditions, such as:

- sufficient relationship values;
- a personal-thread stage;
- rain;
- a recent building move;
- a Light-state threshold;
- a certain keepsake;
- a rare Moon event;
- the visitor having heard an earlier contradiction.

## 37.4 Continuity paradoxes

An NPC may appear inside *Just One More* soon after being seen elsewhere.

If challenged, they may say:

```text
"You saw me outside?"

"No."

"That was probably my shadow."
```

The game does not need to resolve whether this is a lie, teleportation, duplicated state or moving time.

## 37.5 Commenting on absences

Residents may note when someone has not appeared in their expected location.

Example:

```text
THE SLEEPLESS BARISTA:
The Apprentice has not been here.

She may have finally finished something.

...

No. That cannot be it.
```

## 37.6 Conversations continue offscreen

When the visitor walks away from an ambient conversation, the residents continue speaking.

Small speech bubbles may continue briefly and become unreadable with distance.

The village should feel as though its conversations do not exist solely for the player.

## 37.7 Geography commentary

NPCs react differently when buildings move.

- The Aspiring Mayor claims every move was approved.
- The Calculator measures impossible displacement.
- The Technomancer blames or congratulates a bug.
- The Citizen is exhausted and resentful because reaching work has changed again.
- The Apprentice becomes distracted by the urban-planning implications.
- The Sleepless Barista reports that the Coffee Shop arrived before he did.
- The Cartomancer may insist the cards predicted the old location correctly.

Dynamic geography should have a substantial ambient dialogue pool because it is one of the village's defining behaviors.

# 38. Dialogue Modifiers

## 38.1 Rain and storms

Rain is usually caused by The Storm Witch skipping, misreading or ignoring part of a potion recipe.

Rain does not fundamentally rewrite every NPC's personality.

It changes:

- ambient lines;
- locations where residents gather;
- visual behavior;
- a limited set of reactions;
- possible Coffee Shop attendance;
- selected lake and Light conditions.

Common village reaction:

```text
"She skipped step four again."
```

## 38.2 Coffee

Coffee is culturally treated as essential.

Its primary canonical effect on Bianca and the visitor is disappointing:

```text
You drink the coffee.

It does not wake you in the slightest.

You would now very much like to sleep.
```

Coffee varieties may change flavor text, gossip availability or tiny narrative details, but they should not become required stat potions.

The running principle is that everyone insists coffee is necessary, while its measurable effect remains questionable.

## 38.3 Light influence

As Light expands, language itself becomes less alive.

Possible effects:

- fewer answer choices;
- shorter and more literal descriptions;
- disappearing metaphors;
- reduced jokes;
- fixed repeat lines;
- objects ceasing to speak;
- portraits losing expressive animation;
- dialogue boxes becoming cleaner, whiter and more symmetrical;
- contradictions being replaced with one approved version;
- the `Remain silent` option disappearing because silence is considered ambiguous.

When Light is pushed back, linguistic variation and ambiguity return.

Dialogue is one of the primary indicators of Light's influence.

## 38.4 Moon influence

A Moon event may cause:

- The Cartomancer to lose or reveal a card;
- The Lakewatcher to remember another possibility;
- Death to pause mid-sentence;
- Ryuk to look upward;
- a resident to say a line they later cannot remember;
- a reflection to answer instead of the person;
- one option to belong to a different version of the conversation.

## 38.5 Ryuk's interference

Ryuk is the most powerful deliberate disruptor of dialogue presentation.

He may:

- sit over an answer;
- drag an option away;
- choose a response;
- cover a portrait;
- walk through the dialogue box;
- knock over The Cartomancer's cards;
- cause the subject to change;
- insert a line visible only to the visitor;
- end a conversation by leaving with an important object.

These events must remain uncommon enough to feel special.

Ryuk's interference should never make an essential choice impossible without providing another route.

# 39. Character Voice Rules

## 39.1 The Cartomancer

Her voice is:

- elegant;
- magnetic;
- dramatic;
- precise when focused;
- suddenly distracted;
- confusing in explanation despite genuine expertise;
- capable of becoming unsettling without becoming dishonest.

Her confusion may be funny, but her power must never be treated as fake.

Example:

```text
THE CARTOMANCER:
The card speaks of a presence beyond the—

Oh.

Did I leave the kettle on?

I do not own a kettle.

Where was I?
```

## 39.2 The Apprentice

She speaks quickly and enthusiastically.

Her dialogue uses:

- consecutive questions;
- interruptions;
- parenthetical thoughts;
- abrupt topic changes;
- references to current studies;
- visible stress underneath cheerful energy.

Her text reveal may be faster than the default, subject to accessibility settings.

## 39.3 The Aspiring Mayor

She speaks as though addressing a public assembly even when one person is present.

Her voice uses:

- ceremonial openings;
- self-correcting titles;
- official terminology with no legal basis;
- heroic framing;
- sincere motivational language beneath the comedy.

Example:

```text
THE ASPIRING MAYOR:
MY DEAR CITIZENS—

Citizen.

Visitor.

Bianca.

Whichever currently applies.
```

## 39.4 The Calculator

He alternates between ordinary speech and visible calculations.

Percentages should support the joke rather than appear in every sentence.

His display may contradict his spoken voice.

```text
THE CALCULATOR:
I am completely calm.

DISPLAY:
PANIC PROBABILITY: 94%
```

## 39.5 The Storm Witch

Her dialogue communicates impulsivity through:

- quick fragments;
- interruptions;
- exclamations;
- immediate reversals;
- thunder cues;
- arguments with the cauldron.

She should not speak permanently in all caps.

## 39.6 The Technomancer

He uses real technical vocabulary in an accessible way.

His humor has two layers:

- a surface joke understandable without programming knowledge;
- an additional technical joke for visitors who recognize it.

Possible subjects include rendering, cache, collision, deployment, sprites, memory, state, bugs and version changes.

## 39.7 The Sleepless Barista

He speaks warmly, softly and with gossip-driven familiarity.

His stories may begin credibly and then confuse:

- visitors;
- sessions;
- future and past;
- dreams and observed events.

He may fall asleep before completing a sentence.

## 39.8 The Lakewatcher

She is poetic, introspective and clear, but often abstract enough to leave others without a response.

She should occasionally become unexpectedly direct to prevent the voice from becoming heavy.

```text
THE LAKEWATCHER:
Every choice creates a reflection
that continues without us.

Also, you stepped on a frog.
```

## 39.9 The Ghost of Bad Decisions

He is not defined only by suffering.

He is:

- a chronic ruminator;
- a comic philosopher;
- observant about consequences;
- capable of finding meaning inside mistakes;
- increasingly aware that bad decisions may still lead to valuable outcomes.

His archive of errors should mix absurd, recognizable and gently bittersweet material.

## 39.10 The Citizen

He speaks literally, evenly and without metaphor.

He rejects:

- irony;
- unverifiable claims;
- symbolic statements;
- ambiguous answers;
- figurative weather descriptions.

Example:

```text
"It is raining cats and dogs."

THE CITIZEN:
It is raining water.
```

His eventual small lie to protect someone should therefore feel enormous.

## 39.11 Death

Death is concise, grounded and usually honest.

He is not constantly theatrical or solemn.

```text
DEATH:
That was a bad idea.

Not fatal.

Nothing here is.

Still bad.
```

He becomes more solemn only when speaking about Light, the village's origin or something he is sincerely protecting.

# 40. Relationship Expression

## 40.1 Affection is shown indirectly

Characters should rarely announce affection through explicit friendship labels.

They demonstrate it through behavior.

| Character | Sign of trust or affection |
|---|---|
| The Cartomancer | Offers a private reading or reveals part of a card she does not show others |
| The Apprentice | Lends a heavily annotated book and trusts the visitor to return it eventually |
| The Aspiring Mayor | Includes the visitor in an important speech or privately asks for real advice |
| The Calculator | Stops calculating the conversation for a short time |
| The Storm Witch | Attempts to follow a recipe recommended by the visitor |
| The Technomancer | Gives the visitor one of his bug creatures |
| The Sleepless Barista | Remembers the visitor's exact coffee order across sessions |
| The Lakewatcher | Shows a deeply personal possibility in the Lake |
| The Ghost | Leaves one decision buried after discussing it |
| The Citizen | Tolerates an object being out of place or tells a small protective lie |
| Death | Adds a personal handwritten note to an official certificate |

## 40.2 Character arcs

Substantial multi-visit arcs are planned for:

- The Citizen;
- The Ghost;
- The Lakewatcher;
- The Cartomancer;
- The Apprentice.

Other principal characters receive smaller but persistent developments.

## 40.3 No fully completed arcs

Character development should not imply that a person has been permanently solved.

Examples:

- The Apprentice may accept one paused study without ceasing to begin new ones.
- The Ghost may leave one decision buried while continuing to ruminate about others.
- The Lakewatcher may accept one unknowable outcome without resolving every alternative.
- The Citizen may tolerate one uncertainty while remaining fundamentally rigid.
- The Cartomancer may reveal part of a truth without learning her own future.

## 40.4 Regression rules

Important emotional progress should not regress at random.

Regression may occur because of:

- later choices;
- support for Light;
- cruelty or dismissal;
- broken trust;
- refusal to acknowledge a previous action;
- a deliberately chosen branch.

Daily jokes and minor details may contradict themselves freely.

## 40.5 Mutually exclusive experiences

A single local visitor history cannot reveal every branch.

Some paths require:

- high trust;
- high irritation;
- support for ambiguity;
- support for Light;
- opposite answers to the same moral question;
- different rare events;
- a reset and a genuinely separate history.

This is intentional.

The game should encourage curiosity about other possibilities without presenting a completion percentage.

# 41. Personal Interaction Threads

## 41.1 Structure

The game may contain small quest-like threads without:

- quest logs;
- map markers;
- exclamation marks;
- mandatory rewards;
- a fixed order for unrelated threads.

The visitor understands what to do through dialogue and memory.

## 41.2 Approved initial examples

- Recover a missing card for The Cartomancer.
- Suggest a new field of study to The Apprentice, only to discover she began every option.
- Attend an Aspiring Mayor speech delivered to an empty square.
- Help The Calculator make an extremely small decision.
- Recover a burned recipe fragment for The Storm Witch.
- Catch or escort a bug creature for The Technomancer.
- Keep The Sleepless Barista awake long enough to finish a story.
- Find the conditional path to The Lakewatcher.
- Rebury one of The Ghost's escaped decisions.
- Leave one object imperfectly aligned in The Citizen's white house.
- Deliver or complete an absurd certificate for Death.

## 41.3 No mandatory thread completion

A visitor may ignore every personal thread and still explore the portfolio.

Threads deepen characters and unlock variations; they do not gate basic navigation unless the gate is itself a secret.

# 42. Bianca's Keepsake Library

## 42.1 Physical archive instead of inventory

Bianca's house contains an initially sparse bookcase or library structure.

Keepsakes obtained through relationships appear physically inside it.

There is no conventional inventory screen for these objects.

The house itself records the visitor's history.

## 42.2 Keepsake behavior

Keepsakes may:

- move between shelves;
- change orientation;
- talk to nearby keepsakes;
- gain new examination text;
- temporarily disappear;
- be borrowed by Ryuk;
- appear before they were officially received;
- react to their original owner entering the house;
- become affected by Light.

Possible message:

```text
Ryuk borrowed this.

Unfortunately, you needed it.
```

## 42.3 No strict capacity limit

The library may expand, create new shelves or distribute objects around the room.

It should feel like a growing personal archive, not a grid of collectible slots.

## 42.4 Cross-character commentary

Residents may react to keepsakes from others.

Examples:

- The Cartomancer notices Death's certificate.
- The Aspiring Mayor finds her commemorative item placed behind a coffee cup.
- The Citizen is disturbed by a bug creature on the shelf.
- The Apprentice attempts to borrow every book at once.
- Death notices an object that belongs in the Cemetery.
- Ryuk denies moving anything while sitting where it used to be.

# 43. Dialogue Interface

## 43.1 Visual design

The standard dialogue box uses:

- a dark purple-black background;
- a decorated but readable magical border;
- the speaker's canonical title;
- a small pixel-art portrait;
- cream or warm white text;
- minimal icons;
- character-specific animations where useful.

The design should remain readable over every region of the map.

## 43.2 Portraits

Principal NPCs receive small portrait sprites with a limited expression set.

Portrait animation may include:

- blinking;
- eye movement;
- tiny mouth or jaw changes;
- cards moving;
- display changes;
- electricity;
- sleep nodding;
- water ripples;
- transparency shifts;
- Light-related loss of expression.

The portrait must not become so detailed that it conflicts with the in-world sprite style.

## 43.3 Typewriter behavior

Text appears with a typewriter effect.

Reveal speed may vary by character:

- The Apprentice: fast and accelerating;
- The Calculator: segmented or interrupted by recalculation;
- The Sleepless Barista: slower with pauses;
- The Citizen: perfectly uniform;
- Death: measured but concise;
- The Storm Witch: rapid during anger;
- The Lakewatcher: calm and flowing.

Accessibility settings must allow the visitor to increase text speed or reveal the current box instantly.

Revealing a box instantly is not the same as skipping the conversation.

## 43.4 Dialogue cannot be skipped as content

The player advances through each dialogue box.

There is no general button that automatically skips an entire conversation or all previously read scenes.

The visitor may leave through the normal interruption system where allowed.

## 43.5 No dialogue log

The game does not provide a full conversation history or permanent transcript.

The visitor is expected to read and remember.

This supports:

- uncertainty;
- one-time lines;
- contradictory recollection;
- attention to language;
- the feeling of an actual conversation rather than a searchable database.

Accessibility needs should instead be addressed through text speed, pause behavior, legibility and sufficient display time.

## 43.6 Optional voice-like sound

The preferred direction includes small synthesized text sounds rather than full voice acting.

Possible character timbres:

| Character | Sound direction |
|---|---|
| The Cartomancer | cards, soft bells, low magical shimmer |
| The Apprentice | fast page flicks and light syllabic ticks |
| The Aspiring Mayor | ceremonial but slightly overconfident tone |
| The Calculator | restrained electronic beeps |
| The Storm Witch | static and soft electrical cracks |
| The Technomancer | metallic clicks and glitch-like blips |
| The Sleepless Barista | warm low tone with occasional yawn-like pauses |
| The Lakewatcher | water drops and glassy resonance |
| The Ghost | soft echo and airy noise |
| The Citizen | clean, uniform and almost affectless tone |
| Death | low, dry and minimal sound |

These sounds are optional pending production feasibility.

They must be individually reducible or disabled.

## 43.7 Light-state interface transformation

Inside illuminated influence, the dialogue box may gradually become:

- brighter;
- cleaner;
- perfectly rectangular;
- less decorated;
- more symmetrical;
- less animated;
- more restrictive in its available options.

The result should look professionally polished and emotionally wrong.

# 44. Dialogue Length and Content Targets

## 44.1 Interaction length

Recommended ranges:

- ambient comment: 1-3 lines;
- object interaction: 1-3 short boxes;
- ordinary conversation: 4-10 boxes;
- meaningful personal scene: 10-25 boxes;
- rare major event: longer only when broken up through choices, movement or environmental change.

Walls of text remain prohibited.

## 44.2 Launch writing targets

Provisional targets:

| Content type | Initial target |
|---|---|
| Deep principal character | 80-150 lines, responses or variants |
| Mid-depth principal character | 50-100 lines, responses or variants |
| Immediate/comic principal character | 40-80 lines, responses or variants |
| Important talking object | 5-20 interaction lines or states |
| Minor object | 1-5 interaction lines or states |
| NPC-to-NPC pairing | 2-6 short scene variants for high-value pairs |
| Geography-change comments | broad shared pool plus character-specific lines |

These are production targets, not content quotas. Quality and reuse conditions matter more than raw count.

## 44.3 Canonical writing language

The game is authored in English first.

Italian localization may be developed after the English version is stable.

Dialogue IDs, state keys and implementation notes should use stable English identifiers.

The Game Bible remains in English to match the canonical game language.

# 45. Dialogue Data Architecture

## 45.1 Data-driven content

Dialogue should be stored outside scene code where practical.

Each dialogue node may define:

- stable ID;
- speaker;
- text;
- portrait expression;
- reveal style;
- sound style;
- conditions;
- available choices;
- state changes;
- relationship changes;
- one-time status;
- interruption behavior;
- follow-up node;
- ambient or full-dialogue mode;
- Light-state variant;
- localization key.

## 45.2 Example conceptual node

```json
{
  "id": "apprentice_browser_tabs_01",
  "speaker": "the_apprentice",
  "text": "How many tabs do you currently have open?",
  "conditions": ["met_apprentice", "apprentice_topic_browser"],
  "choices": [
    {
      "id": "three_tabs",
      "text": "Three.",
      "effects": ["set:visitor_claim_tabs=3", "trust:+1"]
    },
    {
      "id": "refuse_count",
      "text": "I refuse to count.",
      "effects": ["familiarity:+1", "curiosity:+2"]
    },
    {
      "id": "silence",
      "text": "[Remain silent.]",
      "effects": ["set:apprentice_answered_for_visitor=true"]
    }
  ]
}
```

The final schema remains a technical implementation decision.

## 45.3 Dialogue selection priority

When several lines are valid, the system should prioritize approximately:

1. unresolved required continuation;
2. one-time rare event;
3. active personal-thread scene;
4. direct reaction to a recent choice;
5. current environmental modifier;
6. relationship-state dialogue;
7. visit-count variation;
8. ordinary repeat pool;
9. persistence or insistence easter egg.

This avoids a random joke preventing an important response from appearing.

## 45.4 Randomness with protection

Random dialogue pools should use protections against immediate repetition.

Possible techniques:

- shuffle bags;
- recent-line exclusion;
- weighted rarity;
- per-session cooldowns;
- one-time and per-visit flags.

The village may feel unpredictable without appearing technically broken in an accidental way.

## 45.5 Contradictory memory storage

The system may store:

- the event the visitor experienced;
- each NPC's current interpretation;
- whether the NPC is knowingly lying;
- whether the village has changed the accepted version;
- whether Light has enforced a single version.

This distinction allows deliberate contradictions rather than uncontrolled continuity errors.

# 46. Canonical Sample Interactions

```text
THE APPRENTICE:
Do websites know when nobody is looking at them?

> Yes.
> No.
> This one does.
> [Remain silent.]

[After a pause.]

THE APPRENTICE:
You took too long.

I have decided the answer is yes.

New question.
```

## 46.2 Walking away from The Lakewatcher

```text
THE LAKEWATCHER:
There is a version of every farewell
in which neither person leaves—

> [Turn and walk away.]

THE LAKEWATCHER:
That version was not this one.
```

## 46.3 A remembered lie

```text
THE APPRENTICE:
You said you had three tabs open.

Your browser disagrees.

I respect both accounts.
```

## 46.4 The Citizen and figurative language

```text
THE CITIZEN:
State the reason for your visit.

> I followed my heart.
> I followed the road.
> I do not know.
> [Remain silent.]

[The first option changes.]

> I followed an organ that cannot navigate.
```

## 46.5 Ryuk removes a choice

```text
THE CARTOMANCER:
Would you like to see what Light showed me?

> Yes.
> No.
> Ask Bianca.

[Ryuk lies down over "Yes."]

THE CARTOMANCER:
He has expressed a professional opinion.
```

## 46.6 Coffee

```text
You drink the coffee.

It does not wake you in the slightest.

You would now very much like to lie down.

THE SLEEPLESS BARISTA:
That means it worked.
```

## 46.7 Light removes ambiguity

Before Light influence:

```text
The door may lead outside.

It remembers doing so once.
```

Under Light influence:

```text
DOOR.

DESTINATION: ROOM 03.

STATUS: FUNCTIONAL.
```

# 47. Dialogue Non-Negotiables

1. Principal conversations must frequently offer meaningful authored choices.
2. Silence is an intentional response.
3. Walking away is usually possible and may be remembered.
4. Bianca may occasionally interfere with an answer.
5. The player cannot enter unrestricted free text.
6. Dynamic options must express character or world state, not arbitrary interface tricks.
7. No visible relationship meters are permitted.
8. Relationship state combines hidden values with explicit event memory.
9. Irritation may persist, but curiosity must never create a permanent punishment trap.
10. The game never labels lies for the player.
11. One-time dialogue is allowed and may remain unrecoverable without a reset.
12. NPCs speak to each other without waiting for Bianca.
13. Light affects language, choice and interface, not only scenery.
14. Coffee does not reliably wake anyone.
15. Ryuk may disrupt conversations but cannot permanently block essential progress.
16. Major emotional progress does not regress randomly.
17. Not every relationship path can be seen in one local history.
18. Keepsakes belong in Bianca's physical library, not a conventional inventory.
19. There is no complete dialogue log.
20. Dialogue content cannot be globally skipped, though the current box may be revealed faster for accessibility.
21. The canonical game language is English.
22. Dialogue must remain concise enough to avoid walls of text.
23. Humor may be technically layered but must remain understandable without specialist knowledge.
24. Contradictions must be intentional or at least fictionally supportable.
25. Everything tells a story. Nothing tells the whole story.
